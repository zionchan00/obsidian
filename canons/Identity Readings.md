	- To me how most people in the West think about the self. The body is a border by which we denote where we end and the 'outside' world begins. The inside of the body and the body differs among individuals but a current of commonality exists across all humans: shared physical machinery and shared emotions.
	- The inhabitants of Java which he studied also utilized an inside/outside dichotomy when discussing the self. The difference is that for the Java islander, on the basis that everyone experiences the same emotions, there is no difference between individuals in the inside. The outside refers to the observed world too. While we believe there to be a relation between the inside and outside where both affect one another, the Java islanders believed the two worlds to have nearly no relation with one another, save for the human which they exist in. The Java islander also has another dichotomy to describe the self - smooth/rough. Java islanders framed individuality and variation as 'roughness', akin to the waves on bodies of water. They wished for their selves to be flat, clean, without variation, and hence without individuality.
	- The second of the views of the self which Geertz brings is from the Balinese society. Just their naming conventions alone hint at the radical differences between us and them. Balinese names are made from three parts. The first is dictated by their order of birth in the family, beginning at first-born and ending at fourth-born. The second is dictated by caste (despite the negative connotations from news of the Hindu caste system, caste is comparatively far less weighty in Bali than it is in India). The third is gender. The net effect of this namign convention is a sense of eternal cyclicality throughout history, the temporarlity of the individual and th epermancne of order from the cycle of first-born, seocnd-born, third-born, fourth-born.
	- Geertz writes that the Balinese are a society which paly-acts in life. Each one of these classes is a mask for the Balinese self to wear. There si a term to describe the shame that a Balinese feels when they 'break chatacter' so to speak. It's the problem of the particular in the universal, the undeniably unique moment that every day of life exists which the social classifier can never accoiunt for. In this concrete moment the individuals int he interaction realize that they are capable of acting differently than their labels. Strong enough socialization has shamed this feeling.
	- The difference is that names in the West do not signal information on one's life other than who they are. Balinese names not only do this, but they also impose socially-enforced expectations on how one is supposed to act. Just like in the West, in larger populations many will have the same name. We may share names, but one Zion Chan has no expectaiton on how another Zion Chan is to act.
	- The Balinese naming convention atleast allows for some degree of differentiation implicit in the name of the individual, but the Javanese names do not shame you for what you are or are not - after-all, they have no idea of the difference between one person and the other, everyone is one and the same, a temporary vessel for the current of emotions which flows through all of us.
	- The last culture to study is the Morrocans. The Morccoan naming convention. designates the place you came from, your religion, etc. The beauty of the Morrocan naming convention is that it simply makes you one of many of that label. The labels are too broad to be limiting in Geertz's eyes, and hence while they help in sorting out the demographic details of one's life, they avoid the downside of restricting identity and providing space for the individual to individuate themselves through interacitons with others, without those others having strong preconceptions of what that individual is like. Yes this woman may be from this city, but thereis such a diveristy of people in that city that one's mind reamisn open. This is a strength which is unqiue to Morroco - as the home of multiple religions, multiple tribes, multiple langauges. This same system in Bali, a monoculture, is restrictive.
