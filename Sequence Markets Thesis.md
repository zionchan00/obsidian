 # Investment Memo: Sequence Markets

**Date:** April 5, 2026 **Stage:** Seed Extension / Pre-Series A **Round:** $4M at $40M cap (25% closed) **Source:** Live founder pitch (Peter, co-founder)

## I. Company Overview

Sequence is a multi-leg trade execution infrastructure layer that packages trades across crypto exchanges, prediction markets, and tokenized asset venues — all settling on USDC rails. The pitch: “Expedia for trading infrastructure.” Developers integrate via API (“easy as Stripe”), structure 10–50 leg trades across asset classes, and Sequence handles routing, execution, and clearing across its colocated server network.

**Founders:**

- **Peter:** Macro trading (FX, TRS, repo, futures), Toronto Stock Exchange colo/low-latency infra team. Father has 20+ years at an exchange.
- **Mohammed:** Waterloo CS, founding engineer at Boardy AI, radar/sonar (Verilog) at Nytec with SpaceX/Boeing parts. Was slated for HRT.

**Team:** ~5 people, mostly part-time quants from Citadel Securities, RBC Capital Markets (NY execution desk), Race Trading. All part-time.

## II. The Bull Case — By Investor Lens

### Josh Wolfe (Lux Capital): “Directional Arrow of Progress”

Wolfe would be the most instinctively drawn to this deal. Here’s why:

- **Atoms + bits convergence.** This isn’t a SaaS wrapper. These founders are doing Verilog hardware programming, physically renting server racks next to exchanges, negotiating colo agreements, and running sub-5ms execution. The moat is _physical_ — server space is finite, exchange relationships take months, and the capital commitment ($50K/server, 5–6 servers per exchange, 20 exchanges) creates a genuine barrier. Wolfe’s thesis that “the future is already here, it’s just not evenly distributed” maps directly: HFT infrastructure exists in TradFi but hasn’t been democratized for crypto/prediction markets/tokenized assets.
    
- **Inevitability thesis.** Peter’s core macro insight is compelling: DeFi is re-centralizing toward TradFi market structure. Prediction markets, tokenized equities, and crypto are converging onto the same USDC rails, creating compounding layers of latency inefficiency. This is a _structural_ trend, not a bet on a single asset class. Wolfe would call this “the directional arrow” — you can disagree on timing but not direction.
    
- **The Wintermute/GSR theft is signal, not noise.** When incumbents steal your strategy after a single pitch meeting, it’s the strongest possible market validation. Wolfe often talks about incumbents’ behavior as the truest signal of whether something matters.
    

**Wolfe’s concern:** The team is young and part-time. He’d want to know if Mohammed is full-time and whether the Citadel/RBC quants are committed or just moonlighting. He’d also push on defensibility — if Jump Trading decides to offer this as a service tomorrow, what stops them?

---

### Graham Duncan: “Do These Founders Have the Map?”

Duncan evaluates founders on the quality of their _mental model_ of the territory they’re operating in — not just intelligence, but orientation.

**What he’d like:**

- **These founders are the native species.** They didn’t read about HFT and decide to start a company. They were _going to build this infrastructure anyway_ — Peter at Goldman’s fake equities team, Mohammed at HRT. They just redirected it into an underserved asset class. Duncan talks about founders who have “the map before the territory” — Peter can describe the exact process of setting up a colocated server (talk to exchange biz dev → talk to beaks/AWS team → configure Verilog hardware → rent rack → physical connection → 2–3 month cycle + $50K + $20K/yr maintenance). That’s not research; that’s lived experience.
    
- **The father’s exchange relationships.** Duncan is deeply attuned to the quality of a founder’s _network capital_. Peter’s father having two decades at an exchange isn’t just a nice-to-have — it’s the reason a two-person team got colocated at exchanges that require $300M/month in volume commitments. This is a form of structural advantage that doesn’t show up on a pitch deck.
    
- **Volume bootstrapping insight.** The fact that they could hit $300M/month minimum volume with ~$25K in assets by running mid-frequency strategies at 1–2ms intervals shows deep domain fluency. Most outsiders would assume you need massive capital to meet exchange minimums. The founders understood that speed × frequency = volume, which is a non-obvious insight.
    

**Duncan’s core concern — and it’s a big one:**

The strategic indecision is a red flag. Duncan writes extensively about founders’ relationship to _choosing_. Peter openly says: “We don’t really know what direction we want to go.” They paused a fundraise mid-round to figure out if they want to be a prop shop or an infra company. Duncan would read this as:

> _“This is a team that has stumbled into an extraordinary position but hasn’t yet developed the leadership maturity to make the hard strategic call. The fact that they raised 25% of their round and then paused is concerning — it signals either lack of conviction or, worse, an inability to commit to a path when the opportunity cost of indecision is highest.”_

He’d also note: the team is entirely part-time except the two founders. That’s a prop trading team structure, not a startup structure. He’d want to see evidence that they’re building an _organization_, not just running a clever trade.

### Keith Rabois: “Barrels vs. Ammunition”

Rabois would be the harshest evaluator here. His framework demands:

1. **Clarity of strategy** — He’d immediately zero in on the prop trading vs. infra debate and call it a disqualifying lack of focus. Rabois famously says you should be able to describe your company’s strategy in one sentence. Sequence can’t do that right now. Are they a trading firm or an infrastructure company? These are fundamentally different businesses with different capital structures, team compositions, margin profiles, and investor bases.
    
2. **Unit economics that work today.** Half a cent per API call is a placeholder, not a business model. They can’t charge bps-per-trade (the actually profitable model) because they don’t have broker-dealer licensing, which is 9 months out. Rabois would say: “You’re telling me your real business model is 9 months away and your current one is a stopgap. That’s not a company, that’s a hobby with an API.”
    
3. **Go-to-market is non-existent.** When pressed on distribution, Peter said “Telegram and Twitter.” Rabois, who obsesses over distribution and operational rigor, would eviscerate this. 170 organic signups from a LinkedIn post is nice but it’s not a repeatable, scalable GTM motion. There’s no sales process, no outbound playbook, no conversion funnel. For a product targeting prosumers with “millions of dollars,” the sales motion should be far more sophisticated.
    
4. **The “barrels” problem.** Rabois defines “barrels” as people who can own and ship entire workstreams autonomously. This team has two full-time founders and a bench of part-time quants from prestigious firms. There are zero barrels beyond the founders. Who’s going to run biz dev with exchanges? Who owns the GTM? Who manages the broker-dealer licensing process? The org has no operational depth.
    

**Rabois’s verdict:** Likely a pass. He’d say the ingredients are interesting but the company is pre-strategic-clarity, pre-real-business-model, and pre-operational maturity. He might revisit if they pick a lane and execute for 6 months.

### Mike Maples (Floodgate): “Is This a Thunder Lizard?”

Maples looks for _inflections_ — moments where a change in technology, regulation, or behavior creates a non-linear opportunity that most people miss. His question: **Is this a pattern-breaking company riding a genuine inflection, or a clever trade masquerading as a startup?**

**The inflection case is strong:**

- Multiple asset classes (crypto, prediction markets, tokenized equities) converging onto shared USDC rails simultaneously — this is a genuine, new structural reality
- Prediction markets just proved the thesis: smart contracts clearing via USDC, creating exploitable latency gaps against underlying assets
- Tokenized assets expanding access globally (Peter’s example: Filipino retail investors accessing US bonds)
- The regulatory window is open — no one has broker-dealer licensing for this yet, the infrastructure layer is wide open

**But Maples would challenge the “startup-ness” of this:**

His biggest concern would be whether this is a _venture-scale_ business or a _very profitable small business_. The TAM math (100K traders × 5K calls/day × $0.005 = $9–12M ARR) is honest but modest. Even at the bps-per-trade model, are we looking at a $100M+ ARR outcome? Or is the real money in proprietary trading, which is a fund, not a startup?

Maples would also flag that the founders’ _instinct_ keeps pulling them back to prop trading. Every time the infra-as-a-service story comes up, Peter pivots to “but we could make more trading ourselves.” That’s not the instinct of someone building a platform company. Maples might conclude:

> _“These are exceptional traders who should probably raise a fund, not a startup round. The VC structure is misaligned with what this team actually wants to do.”_

**The Thunder Lizard scenario:** If tokenized assets genuinely explode (trillions in tokenized equities, bonds, commodities all trading on-chain), and Sequence becomes the execution/clearing layer beneath every trading platform (like the Etherfi partnership suggests), then this is the picks-and-shovels infrastructure play for the next financial system. That’s a $10B+ outcome. But it requires the founders to _commit_ to being an infra company and stop flirting with prop trading.

### Brett Caughran: Market Structure & Macro Positioning

Caughran would evaluate this primarily through the lens of market structure evolution and whether the macro timing is right.

**What he’d affirm:**

- The DeFi-to-CeFi re-centralization thesis is well-supported. Order books are winning over AMMs. Centralized exchanges are growing faster than DEXs. The structural trajectory mirrors TradFi evolution.
- The latency arbitrage across fragmented crypto venues is a _known, proven_ alpha source in TradFi (Reg NMS created it in equities). The same dynamic is now emerging across 20+ crypto exchanges, prediction markets, and tokenized venues — but with _worse_ fragmentation and _fewer_ sophisticated players. Classic market structure alpha.
- Cross-asset convergence on USDC rails is a genuine macro inflection. The ability to trade BTC on Coinbase, hedge on Hyperliquid, and express a directional view on Polymarket — all in one atomic transaction — is new and valuable.

**What he’d challenge:**

- **The alpha decay problem.** Peter himself admitted: “It’s not really sustainable because you have to constantly chase alpha.” HFT alpha in TradFi compressed dramatically as more players entered. The same will happen in crypto. The window where a 5-person team can compete with Jump Trading is _finite_ — maybe 12–24 months. The question is whether they can build a durable infra business before the prop trading edge erodes.
    
- **Competitive dynamics are vicious.** Wintermute and GSR already copied one of their strategies from a single pitch meeting. If the alpha is that easy to replicate from a slide deck, the moat around the _strategies_ is thin. The moat has to be in the _infrastructure_ (colo network, exchange relationships, API integrations) — but the founders keep being pulled toward the strategies.
    
- **The volume is impressive but potentially misleading.** $500M/month in volume sounds massive, but at 1–2ms trade frequency, it’s mostly self-referential churn from their own mid-frequency strategies. Organic customer volume is still near zero (they launched 2 weeks ago with 170 signups but no meaningful revenue from external users).

## VI. The Meta-Observation

The single most important thing about this pitch is what _wasn’t_ said clearly: **Sequence’s founders haven’t decided if they’re building a company or running a fund.** Every major concern — strategic indecision, part-time team, placeholder business model, thin GTM — stems from this unresolved identity question.

The infrastructure is genuinely impressive. The market timing may be perfect. The founders clearly have the technical chops and domain knowledge. But as Graham Duncan would say: _the quality of a decision is determined by the quality of the frame around it._ Right now, the frame is blurry.

The most important question for any investor considering this deal isn’t about market size or unit economics. It’s: **Do Peter and Mohammed want to be founders or traders?** Everything else follows from that answer.
