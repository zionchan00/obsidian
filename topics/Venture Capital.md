What does a good venture capitalist do? We can imagine that a finite pool of good startups exists every single year that have the makings of a 3x fund returner at exist
- You first need to be aware that these startups exist before you can start analyzing them
- You then need to be able to identify them for what the yare
- You then need to convince them to take your money
- You then (should) do whatever is in your power to shepherd them to success
 How to succeed as a VC?
- On a spectrum we can imagine two ways of finding great companies: thesis-driven and human-driven. the first is much more top-down, you do research ahead of time and come to a strong opinion on what you're looking for in the market and find the founders that best fit it. the second one is bottom-up, you spend less time doing research proactively and more time letting your network steer you good quality founders who will pitch you their vision of the world and your end view is much more determined by them than it is by you
	- this spectrum is messy in that even the most thesis-driven investor has their views affected by founders in the market; if you're looking to invest in frontier science companies, your views on what's possible are usually shaped by the scientists/founderes doing research (unless you're advanced enough to know what's possible at the frontier yourself, but this usually means you'll incubation the company to capture a higher return on your knowledge vs letting someone else capitalize on it)
	- one way to tell which firm is which is by the sectors they focus on and also the depth of knowledge needed to get up to date on that sector. deeply technical verticals almost always require a thesis-driven approach because you're taking on technology risk in addition to market risk and human risk, while less technical verticals are usually only the latter two. technical ideas take a lot longer to learn vs learning about consumer/enterprise software trends, which any human being can get up to speed on very quickly
- Being able to identify a breakout company for what it is is difficult, even when it's right in front of you. Just look at Bessemer VP's Anti-Portfolio
- Even if you identify them as a winner, if you're not alone in doing so then you'll have to compete for a limited pool of equity with others. The more equity you want the more control you have over the valuation terms, but the founder will expect more from you (either higher valuation or more of your help, which I'll define below). 	- At incorporation, the only data points people have to judge how good your company with is the human beings on the team/investors backing the team and their respective histories. if the company manages to build a successful product and begin generating revenue, those serve as additional data points that everyone uses to try to predict how successful the company will be in the future. This prediction game matters a lot because if the company is a hit (and people are thinking about the upside only), 95%+ of the equity will be generated after the seed/series a. that's a huge reward if things go right but everyone's thinking about the massive unknowns and the failure stories they've heard too; investors, people you want to hire, customers, your social network, etc want as much info as possible before they pick you as the winner. so you want to increase the quantity and quality of data points for people to judge whether you're a winner or not as #1 priority. You can do this by picking the right problem, one where it's obvious the market really needs you. Then you'll be able to show investors there are many customers who want (semi-public source of legitimacy) your product (non-public source of legitimacy) and use it to capture high-quality investors (public source of legitimacy). often your customers aren't big companies though, so you can't use them as a source of legitimacy early on. if you're really lucky you and your fellow early colleagues are very well-credentialed, so that's a good one to have early on. but for many founding teams historically, investors were the most important source of legitimacy. good investors help you get everything: they make hiring, selling, and funding through their brand. but what I was saying I underestimated earlier was how important investors were for the founder personally. because if the founder is low on all sources of legitimacy, they're surrounded by FUD everywhere: from friends and family who are well meaning, customers who don't see the potential of their product, people in their network who politely say no to joining their startup, etc. the public world is almost a place of shame for them as a reminder of how little faith people who don't get it have in them. so there's a personal element for the founder, a need for validation, that I didn't consider. Its an intense source of pride to say 'Sequoia, who backed x and y companies before, also believes in ME'

How to succeed as a newcomer to the VC game?
- I define a newcomer as someone with no brand nor knowledge (differentiated analysis or advice-giving) advantages. The broad advice is to get both because that's how you'll win the most competitive deals. But this requires a certain penchant for publicity which some people by nature dislike, and it's more than possible to generate amazing returns without having to do both. The first is to go extreme on the publicity front
Part of the reaosn that the reflexivity of self-belief isn't didscussed openly is that neither the venture capitalists nor founders wish to admit that contribution of investor branding to founder self-belief. For the founder, other than historical accuracy in letting other founders know what it's really liek to run a company, there's no benefit in telling the truth that the belief of outsiders strengthened your own conviction. That sounds weak, much better to pain the picture that all along you knew that this company was going to be a big hit. For the venture capitalist, it may initially seem like a good thing to make it known that your brand is so strong that it inspires zealus self-belief in the founders you back that leads them to success. But strong brands are by definition already known - this adds nothing to the conversation.
