		- In *The Unity and Diversity of the Natural Sciences*, De Koninck takes aim against modern natural science for losing its way. He believes that this is caused by the artificial walling off of philosophy and its questions as a separate, unrelated study that is unnecessary for science. What I find problematic are both his arguments for why there is no distinction in nature between the two and why the science needs philosophy. I will first lay out his main arguments before expanding on what I find problematic about them.
		- De Koninck first compares science and philosophy. Drawing support from Aristotle and Bertrand Russell as representative of philosophy and science respectively, he believes that both are alike in how they begin their analysis of phenomena - from vague generalities to specific particulars. To his dismay science has strayed from this however, the first of many sins. Instead of trying to understand what it means to be alive, the scientist chooses to replace the living being with a symbolic representation, choosing not to answer such questions and instead focus on quantitative predictions of behavior. In its desire to be exact from the beginning, science gives up on understanding the qualitative. This desire has more impacts than a bias for the numeric. Comparing his high school textbook to Aristotle's *De Cœlo*, the latter attempts to understand the motion of bodies in general while the former explains local motion only. In doing De Konick believes the differences between local motion and motion in general are overemphasized compared to what they have in common, leading to a dismissal of those differences overtime and confusion on the nature of reality. He believes that we must return to the tradition because the hardest and most important questions remain unanswered in favor of practical ones. "The only way of achieving true success in such investigation is by deeper and deeper understanding of what was at first only vaguely known" (pg.149). Questions on vague generalities are left for the philosophers and as science increasingly focuses on the particular, the chasm between the two only grows. The scientist increasingly finds the philosophers comparatively vague and their qualitative understanding of nature uncompelling, creating a wall between the two that De Koninck believes artificial and should not exist.
		- There are two kinds of arguments De Koninck puts forth in favor of this. Firstly that the distinction has no natural basis and only exists in our minds. Secondly that the distinction worsens science. On the first, the distinction is commonly held because there is too much knowledge in philosophy and science, forcing individuals to specialize.  Overtime the grand project of knowledge accumulation has created a library so vast that nobody can consume it all in their lifetime. Death forces us to be selective and specialize in a few fields. As a result people choose to either learn science deeply or philosophy deeply, creating a false perception that one must choose between the two. Implied in this view that there is no natural barrier between the two is that philosophic knowledge is scientific and/or scientific knowledge philosophic, because for a barrier to exist, the two must have some distinction by nature such that they do not overlap.

	- Q77 A1. Why are the powers not the essence of the soul?
		- Every power is made of a pair, potentiality and actuality, that belong to the same genus. We know that the essence of the soul belongs to the genus of substance. But the actuality of the soul, the powers being actualized, do not belong to the genus of substance. it's only God that has power as essence
		- If the souls essence was its power, then it would always be actualized. But this is false - the soul is a first actuality, not a second actuality. The soul has the power of sight and can use it to see but it is not always seeing. The exception is movement - the soul is always actualizing the power of movement while the body it is with is alive. Therefore it isn't accurate to say that the powers are the essence
		- Aquinas agrees that if the powers of the soul are not substantial, they are accidental - more specifically they lay in the category of quality. But accident could also be thought of in terms of the 5 universals, and under this the powers fall into the category of properties because they don't belong to the essence of the soul but are necessary consequences of the soul's essential principles
	- Q77 A2. Whether there are several powers of the soul?
		- Aquinas argues that the powers of the soul are distinguishable, plural, not united as one. He believes that a hierarchy of being exists where the higher one is, the more unified their soul. Man is the lowest on the spiritual hierarchy with God at the top; so he therefore has a plurality of powers while God has a single one that corresponds with his Essence
	- Q77 A3. Can we tell the difference between the powers through their acts and objects?
		- Aquinas argues yes we can tell the difference by their objects. Each object activates a corresponding single power - e.g things we see and hear activate the sensory power's sense of sight and hearing. The exception is the power of common sense but Aquinas deals with this through a hierarchy of objects, where the object of the sense of sight is the visible sensible but the object of the sense of common sense is the sensible in general, which includes the sense of sight

- Double Effect
	- Is it a good reason to kill for self-preservation just because it's natural to desire keeping the body alive?
	- Aquinas believes that actions have two kinds of impacts: intended and accidental. The morality of an action is dependent on what is intended, not what is accidental. Because it is natural to want to not die, Aquinas believes it is ok to intend to preserve one's self through murder in self-defense. But the morality of the act also depends on its proportion to the intention. If the damage dealt to the attacker was beyond what was needed for self-preservation, the act is no longer moral. Those who fight for the public good are forgiven for murder by Aquinas if they do so for the common good, i.e soldiers who kill their battlefield foes and judges who execute criminals. These members of the public have committed evil however if 'private animosity' 'moved' them to the act.
	- I can think of two ways to define 'intended' and accidental. One way to see the relation is that you wouldn't blow up the fat man in any circumstance other than to escape the cave. The 'intended' effect is the final reason for all that we do in a scenario, the why. The accidental is the 'how'. In this definition Aquinas justifies murder in self-defense by the ends justifying the means (with the caveat that the means must be proportionate to the end). Another way to interpret the two is 'intended' as what one has considered when making a decision and accidental as that which was not in consideration. In this definition a tension is revealed in Aquinas' analysis of self-defense. Self-defense is 'natural', man is 'bound to take care of one's own life than of another's. We could go further and say that self-defense is instinctive, unthinking. When we are punched, immediately the desire to punch back shoots up. At the same time Aquinas wishes for us to rationally consider whether the means are in proportion to the end of the self-preservation. The responsibility of the defender to think this through moves the damage inflicted to the attacker from the realm of the accidental to the intentional. If we have thought through the fact that we are killing someone, why are we not wrong to do so? Is it also not natural to wish to preserve the life of others, to be free of murder? We could always choose to maim instead of kill, but the reason why people aim for the center of the body is because the instinct for self-preservation kicks in, we don't have time to think about how to neutralize instead of kill unless we're heavily trained. To summarize, I think a tension exists between the 'instinct' and 'rational consideration' which Aquinas touches on but not fully enough. Is murder forgiven only because it's instinctual? What about cases where it was instinctual but in hindsight avoidable?
	- It may be natural to wish to preserve one's life but in murder we deny the murdered the ability to repent and improve their souls so that when they reach the afterlife, they go to Heaven instead of Hell. As a Christian, Aquinas should have considered the cost of robbing someone the chance of atonement. If the defender is Christian, at worst they lose their presence in the temporal world and ascend to Heaven.
