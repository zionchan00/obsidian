	- ### 1. A Theory of Development

If you take a toy from the hands of a one-month old infant, the baby stares off into space and quickly forgets it ever existed. It's as if the baby didn't understand that it had the toy in its hands, that the toy was theirs, and whatever is out of sight doesn’t exist anymore for him. But try to take a toy from the same baby two years later and the baby will cry, scream, and chase you around the room until you give it back.

Or fill up a long, tall, beaker with water and pour into another beaker that’s shorter and wider. It's the same amount of liquid in the second beaker but newborns will believe that the second beaker has less water because the water level is shorter. And if you pour the liquid back, they'll tell you that you created more water. Kids around the age of seven will usually reason that by pouring the water back into the other beaker and the water level returning to its old amount, the amount of water has not changed across the two containers. Kids who are eleven older won’t have to physically pour the water back and forth to understand it hasn’t changed - they can reason that the water has properties which makes its appearance different depending on its environment.[1](#footnote-1)

The commonality between the liquid experiment and the toy experiments is that newborns believe all of their perceptions wholeheartedly. But most adults clearly don't, and we can see that kids who are older don’t either. 

What enables more developed individuals to do this? To think about something requires taking a step back from it, and it's only with this distance that disagreement happens. In the case of newborns they think they **_are_** their perceptions so they cannot contradict them. We can can think **_about_** our perceptions because they are **_separate_** from us. So it seems that as we develop we change how we define what is ‘I’ and what is ‘not’, what is ‘subject’ and what is ‘object’ to us. 

And this is the theory which this is going to be explained for the rest of this essay - our development is the redefining of the boundary of subject and object, which changes how we relate and make sense of everything in the world. This is Donald Kegan’s theory of psychological development he calls **_‘meaning-making’_**.

---

The above example makes it clear how this boundary impacts your ability to reason. But the way we relate to everything-that-is-not-us also changes our emotions. We’ve all met people who can’t say no and people who suffer from separation anxiety before, and I think most of us understand why they act this way - they define themselves by their relationships, making it painful or not even possible to disagree with the people around them. 

In theory, it should also impact our morals[2](#footnote-2). Going back to the water-in-a-beaker experiment, tell the three different aged-boys the same thought experiment. Who’s worse: someone who accidentally destroys 5 plates or someone who destroys a plate on purpose. The youngest child will believe the first and the other two the latter, which fits exactly into how they make sense of the world - ‘don’t you see’, says the youngest one, ‘he broke **_more_** plates’. Because motivations can contradict perceptions - we can debate the motivation and attribute multiple motivations to the same action - it makes sense that the newborn can’t understand it.

This makes clear the moral difference between the newborn and the two older boys, but another evolution takes place between the two older ones. Teach a seven-year old the golden rule, ‘do unto others as you would have done unto them’, and how do they apply it when they get bullied by older kids? They tell you how they can’t wait to be older so they can bully younger kids. ‘That’s just the way it is, it was their turn then and it’ll be our turn soon’. Twisted as it this is to us, there IS some sort of logic here which we can make sense of. It’s kinda like karma; if something bad happens to you, there should be something good (the power to bully others) which cancels it out. 

What makes us disagree with the seven-year old is that we don’t believe bullying others is a reward. It’s bad to us to make others feel bad and if you communicate it to the kid, he doesn't understand that because his brain doesn’t have the capacity to model how others feel entirely yet. He can understand that others are different, that they are separate agents, but he cannot **_imagine_** how they feel. And if he did, the wave of negative emotion would probably prevent him from doing what he wants to do.

Of course researchers and educators will try telling the kids that it sucks getting bullied. But the facts are uselesss without the correct interpretation. The kids won’t ‘get it’. 

And that's why I say the morality of a seven-year old is similar to but is not karma. Karma as an ethic can be applied to others - ‘something bad happened to him, I hope something good happens to him too’ because the emotions that support karma as a morality are supported by the ability to empathize and imagine how others feel.

This change between the seven-year old and eleven-year old is the evolution between the concrete operational stage and the formal operational stage according to Piaget. In the concrete stage the set of possible outcomes is what is in front of me. Ask me to imagine if my little brother had a dragon and I would reply, ‘well he doesn't have one though’. 

But something magical happens overtime. After a few years the child learns to think in hypotheticals. They begin to be able to reason about types of objects, classes, and the properties that all objects which belong in this class have. They begin to understand that the current word is just one of many worlds that could have existed , and their perspective is also one of many other perspectives that exist. The ability to think so abstractly is what allows older kids to skip physically pouring the water back and forth and just logic-it-out instead, but also to consider other people’s motivations in their actions. This change creates a further change in the way we define ourselves and what isn’t themselves. 

It’s not only the improvement of mental machinery but also experience with the world that improves our cognition. After seeing enough real-world experiments we naturally begin to come-up with theories of how things work, so it’s a mix of both nature and nurture. And that’s what I want you to takeaway from the first part of this essay - a delicate dance between mental development, external stimuli, and something magical changes how we make sense of the world. While Kegan doesn’t make the claim that meaning-making is the one theory to explain human behaviour, what makes my analysis of his work different from [David Chapman’s](https://vividness.live/developing-ethical-social-and-cognitive-competence), the most popular explanation of Kegan’s work, is that I make it clear there is something more fundamental than meaning-making through the above example. Because Piaget did not study reasoning capabilities in adults, Kegan stops all mention of the development of logical reasoning after the age of 12. 

This process doesn’t always go right

I don’t know what is that thing which is ‘more fundamental’ and the literature doesn’t either. My guess is that it’s biology, and it’s likely no coincidence that Kegan makes 0 mention of biology and development, choosing to focus entirely on how our social environment nurtures us instead. I’m going to come back to this later on in the third part, where I explain in what ways our social environment has broken down to and link them back to common pathologies in our psychology. Before you can understand how development breaks down however I want to go deeper into the different stages of development so we can have a vision for what ‘good development’ look like. 

### 2. What Development Looks Like

The evolution over time is that the world gives us feedback that our current understanding of ourself isn’t working anymore, and we need to adapt. And because we’re learning by mistakes, it’s natural for us to overcorrect and believe that we can find a new principle to define us, before we’re taught by the world again that we need to change. Along each stage, we take something which we made a defining principle of who we are, realize that it’s something we have, and then search for another defining principle.

Nothing is automatic - everything is **learned**[3](#footnote-3). That’s why the newborn believes everything is subject - it is everything, there is no ‘I’, the only reality that matters is its own. It has to begin generating knowledge by touching everything, smelling everything, and tasting everything. The baby sounds like it’s reliant on its senses (to me it seem like the baby thinks through its senses) and that’s because the newborn would not believe in a difference between its senses and it. 

The problem with believing you are your senses however is that it leaves the baby incapable of having the idea of an ‘object’ or a ‘concept’. To have these ideas a baby has to be able to think about its senses and you can’t do that when you are your senses, which is why when you hide a toy from a newborn, it forgets it immediately. Newborns cannot understand that objects are permanent.

But something changes overtime. Just watch the short video below, everything I write above is clearly on display.

Somewhere in development the baby realized that the data from its senses was not it - its perception was made an object for it to manipulate with its brain. The baby needs a new organizing principle, and it instead believes it is its impulses. 

As the baby begins to learn about object permanence from the world, it then becomes easy for the baby to begin understanding the idea of cause and effect because it realizes there are permanent things with properties attached to them. But as the water-experiment from the first part of this essay shows, it takes continued experimentation and education for them to get the properties for different objects and classes right. Kids at this stage will believe that high temperature on thermometer causes fever, not the other way around. Their interests tend to be in the fantastical and unrealistic as a result because their understanding of reality is so inaccurate.

Object permanence also allows the child to realize that there is a constant self which has emotions, and it’s further helped in development by the struggle it has with its impulses. In a dream world the kid would get whatever it wanted, but in a proper environment, this shouldn’t happen. It’s the role of the family and extended caretakers to tell the kid no to its impulses. This makes the kid realize that it isn’t its impulses, that a new organizing principle is needed.

But none of these developments are smooth. Evolution here is tricky, it requires both the child and the environment to go in the right direction because we’re asking the kid to literally change their worldview. The old self will always be tugging away, the one that says to follow your impulses blindly. But thanks to the voice of adults, kids begin to realize they can disagree and fight those impulses. An idea of ‘right’ is formed for the first time such that even when adults aren’t supervising their kids, they can begin restraining themselves.

Think about the fears kids have at this age. Monsters that devour them in their dreams, fear of the dark, etc. What if these monsters represent the child’s impulses and the fear comes from the inability to control them? 

After repeated attempts to get others to do what they want and lecturing from others, the child learns that other people have impulses and most importantly, that their impulses seem to be ordered by different desires. The child both acknowledges other human beings exist, no longer believing in its old worldview and separating them from himself, and instead it begins organizing itself by its desires.

---

The child may understand that others exist but she is not capable of ‘empathy’ at this point because its understanding is mediated by a theory of competition. The child understands everyone has their own desires but when it comes to conflict between them, she sees no reason why one person should give up their desire for the other. When the child feels guilt, it isn’t because of the harm that is caused to someone but because of the harm that is done to them.

On the level of cognition, children here are in the concrete operational stage from the first part of the essay. This gives them the ability to reason about object permanence and understand that a human being exists even if they are out of sight. But they struggle mentally to think about a human when they aren’t there. 

With the idea of object permanence and the permanence of the self, along with the timing that the child goes to school, she begins to understand that they play a ‘role’ which changes in different environments. With her parents she’s their kid, with their teachers she's a student, and with her friends she’s a friend. We can notice role construction come into their lives as a realization from the games that kids like to play: role-playing games! At the beginning of this stage kids will enjoy roleplaying as fantasy characters but as time goes on, they are increasingly interested in roleplaying as real-world adults, e.g cops and robbers. 

Desire as an organizing principle works if the child is spoiled. But as soon as they enter school, there are one too many competing desires for everyone to be satisfied, and they chive enough feedback and the repeated teachings from adults around them tell them that they. [4](#footnote-4)

They begin to understand that they have a relationship with others around them. They have obligations, and feel guilty if they don’ t them. The sudden development of the feeling of guilt leads them to overcompensate often, swinging hard enough that they make their relationships everything. 

But inevitably they run into a situation where their obligations to other people conflict with one another, either because they compete or because the person overpromises. This become especially problematic because the person who organizes themselves by their relationships lacks a higher-order principle to settle conflicts. They instead choose their obligations on the basis of whether the people who they promise are physically or temporally proximate because they define their identity by these people. 

In cognitive development these people are in the early stages of the formal operational stage. They increasingly become interested in the hypothetical, they’re capable of doing more advanced from software logic and reasoning through thought experiments. 

Either something goes wrong or the feeling of being adrift that comes from consistent identity change leads the human to burn out and realize something has to change - they need a way to determine how to prioritize their obligations. Or perhaps the teenager becomes educated, and is capable of coming to realize that all that they care about either belongs to or is supported by a greater group which enables the functioning of the relationships they care about. They slowly open themselves to a new organizing principle - that which all of their relationships exist within. The company, tribe, the city, the country. An ‘I’ begins to form, which rigidly follows these values of these groups

### 3. What drives progress in moral reasoning?

Reading these experiments made me think about a conversation I had once with the TA of a philosophy class. Students were given the following thought-experiment. ‘Imagine you are a weapons chemist with a family that is both starving and deathly sick. Your only choices are to join a weapons company or face state imprisonment under suspicion that you’re a traitor to the enemy and your family dies. What is the morally right choice and why?’. Some of the students responded, ‘Canada has food stamp programs and we have universal healthcare. So the man can go to jail without harming his family’. Obviously, they missed the point of a thought experiment. And they failed the question.[6](#footnote-6)

If students can’t imagine a different future in a scenario which has nothing to do with them, I don’t believe they’ll do any better in an actual ethical scenario where they are personally involved. It’s very common for young people to shoplift, mostly out of the logic that nobody was harmed. ‘Well, imagine if someone WAS harmed?’. ‘Cheating is ok because the other person didn’t find out, it’s all good.’’ Well, what if they did?’. There’s this inability to think about the counterfactual and how that should play into our moral judgements. I’m just frankly in a state of shock. There are actual human beings out there who cannot imagine hypotheticals - they cannot understand how others feel. 

They walk among us, we let them vote, we treat them like any other adult when they’re basically a 12-year old in development. And when they hurt other people, do bad things, we act surprised. 

So if you can relate to the story of development I’ve written about in the first two sections, you’ve no doubt looked at the past and thought, man I can’t believe I was like that, I wasn’t even a full human being back then. 

It then becomes impertinent to me that we become more thoughtful about how to get more people to ‘level up’. We can do this by understanding how people usually progress through the stages.
