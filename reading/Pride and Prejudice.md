- One way to understand the relationships of Pride and Prejudice would be to place them on a continuum with pure 'romantic' love on one end and 'acquisitive' love on the other end. Relationships of the first kind are defined by the attraction lovers have for one another's beings, a partnership formed on the basis of the enjoyment of the other's company - union between sexual desire and friendship. Relationships placed on the extreme of the other end are created for the gain of physical assets or social capital. In the differences in happiness between Jane and Bingley, she who cares not for the financial capital gained and he not for the social capital lost, and Mr.Collins and Charlotte, she who marries only for financial gain and he who marries purely to have a wife, it seems Austen favours romantic love.
- The flavour of romanticism Austen favours is different from the romanticism described above, however, in that the happiest couples are ones where not only physical and emotional attraction exist, but the united couple are also rich enough in financial and social capital not to deal with the stresses that come from poverty and the feeling of inferiority. For marriage is is not only the union of the soul but also the union of financial and social standing. Furthermore the possible gain that could come from marrying 'upwards' or the negative ramifications for marrying 'downwards' extend not only to the individuals directly in the relationships but also their family, who also stand to either gain or lose from the relationship. The ideal partner for one's soul, the most romantic partner, may not be the best possible partner anymore when one considers the duty one has towards their family and their own material and social well-being. Another opposition could be created then between 'desire' and 'duty'. While Stendhal's ideal love is one that is purely romantic and ignorant of duty (i.e Mme. De Renal failing her marital duties is viewed sympathetically because her husband fails to satisfy her romantically), Austen's ideal loves (Elizabeth and Darcy with Jane and Bingley ranking below them) are not only physically attracted and compatible in terms of personality, but are also materially and socially so well off that neither are ever of concern to both. Where Stendhal is unsympathetic to the norms of marriage and virtue of his time, rushing through the barriers erected by lame bugmen too scared pursue their passions no matter the price, the dance between Austen's heroes and their partners is so slow and modest that they have difficulty deciphering whether their attraction is reciprocated.
- The most Stendhalian character in Pride and Prejudice, Lydia, is unable to find her counterpart for it is revealed that her partner marries her only for material gain, and is unable to find happiness in her marriage, forever dependent on her prudent sisters for handouts. Even in Elizabeth and Darcy's relationship, the couple whose personalities fit the best, Elizabeth only begins to find Darcy attractive after visiting his estate and fantasizing being its owner. Austen seems to think the Stendhalian ideal not only unadmirable, but also impossible.
- In spite of Mr.Bingley's considerable wealth, praised by the community around Jane, Jane herself never cares for his assets. Jane's attachment to Bingely is so metaphysical that she fails to compliment his looks, only his manner and nature, after their first meeting, despite the narrator and everyone else at the ball seeing the former (along with his wealth) to be what makes him a most attractive bachelor. Bingley's love for Jane is so pure that what prevents him from pursuing her is not the social repercussions, but Darcy convincing him that she does not reciprocate her feelings.
- Wickham and Lydia are harder to put within this frame. Wickham, who is impoverished and is alone in the world, has no obligations to others, no duty to the family. His indebtedness makes him view marriage in terms of financial gain which is why had it not been for Darcy's interference, he wouldn't have married Lydia for the payout was too low.  Even though Lydia does have a family which she is expected to consider, she cares much more for her satisfaction of her desires than her duties, lowering the social standing of her family by eloping and marrying someone of low financial/social status, and even disregarding her own social standing in doing so.
- Most relationships don't sit on the continuum but contain parts of both. From Elizabeth's reaction to Charlotte's marriage, a reader likely believes that she believes in immaterial love. In chapter 18 of part 1, she focuses on whether Jane would be happy with Mr. Bingley while Mrs.Bennet is focused on the material gain. But the 'turning' of her feelings towards Mr.Darcy aren't purely immaterial. The first part where she begins to find him attractive is when she visits his estate and fantasizes about the possibility that the beautiful grounds before her were hers. The improvement of his manners towards her ilk increased his attractiveness, but so was the massive amount of money he spent patching up Wickham and Lydia's relationship by practically bribing Wickham to marry her. The practical assistance he provided her family mixes the lines between desire and duty. It showcases generosity of personality, dedication to Elizabeth. But it could also be read as a part of the inescapability of practicality. Just like in real life, it's not clear how much weight could be assigned to which side.
- What does it mean that the happiest relationships are Jane and Mr.Bingley and Elizabeth and Mr.Darcy? Both are THE bachelors par excellence, wealthy and in good social standing. At the same time their personalities fit well with their female counterparts. It seems she believes a good partnership satisfies both desire and duty. In contrast to Stendhal's the Red and the Black, where desire is so strong that a wife abandoning her duties as a wife for the tutor of the family is admirable. Stendhal laughs at conventional restraints, daring his characters to break them for individual happiness; Austen seems to be the restrained defender of conventional morality, the voice of calm reason.
- The marriage which seems to be in the lowest standing is either Mr.Bennet and Mrs.Bennet, who have obviously very little synergy with one another or Lydia and Wickham. Mr.Bennet married Mrs.Bennet for her 'good humor' and looks, Lydia and Wickham are reliant upon handouts from the other two relationships to survive. Very little was written about whether their personalitiies were a good fit or not in the first place
- Pride
	- What is pride? Pride refers to the parts of our being and history which we love the most. The more we love this part of our being, the stronger the attachment, the more pleasure we derive from the fact that this thing is part of our being. We could love most our achievements in school, a particular skill we have, a personality trait, etc. What we take pride in is what we consider to be the most lovable and the best parts of ourselves because you don't love the parts of yourself which you believe to be inferior or middling. You love the parts of yourself which you believe to either best represent you OR best represent the you who you wish to be
		- We should be proud of parts of our identity which are standalone 'good' or 'healthy'. If you're proud of how great of a friend you are to others, the happiness you derive from this being part of your identity further pushes you to think 'im someone whos a good friend' and hence you keep being a good friend. if you're proud fo something you did at work and you consider it a big deal, you're going to keep working hard and try evne better next time because what you're proud of matters to you. so to me the impact of pride is it's a positive feedback loop, and whether pride is good or bad depends on what is being hammered into you
		- The downside of excessive pride for the indivdiual is that even if you're proud of something that's good, because pride is happiness for something the way it is, it can prevent change. I.e you're always proud of what has happened, not what is going to happen; proud of who you are, not who you will be. It's always backwards looking. It can prevent you from being critical because you believe you're already good at it.
