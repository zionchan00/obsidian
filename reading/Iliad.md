- Violence in the Iliad
	- What Homer uniquely understands about violence in the Iliad to me is the obligation the dead impose on the living to keep fighting, creating conflicts which only intensify. One can learn of this by studying the fights that take place throughout the story. As the Iliad progresses and the body-count increases, increasingly the fightings are motivated by revenge.
	- Hector's skill in combat brings him great glory. But it also dooms his family to slavery and death, for no Greek who's close one was killed by Hector would have mercy on his close ones.
	- The increasing body count changes not only the motivations behind conflict but also the character of the warriors. Until Book 18 most killings were done without boasting or words exchanged between the duellers. In the books preceding Achilles' taking on the battlefield however both sides begin to take deep pleasure in the murder of another, inspiring more conflict.
	- The obligation exists not only in the form of an obligation to continue the conflict but an obligation to either strip the body of your enemy or defend the body of your comrade.

- What can we glean about the Iliad's meaning rom the structure of the story
	- Structure - book begins with Agamemnon and Achilles feuding and Achilles withdrawing from combat, the tide of war switching back and forth between the Greeks and Trojans, Trojans nearly win the war and Patrocles heads into battle and dies, Achilles comes out finally and kills Hector, and then gives Hector's body back to his father
		- To be more specific the very first lins are an invisible narrator requesting the Muses tell the story of Achilles' wrath. Achilles is but one of the parties in conflcit however - the other party, Agamemnon, is also wrathful, as shown a few lines later in his reaction to Calchas' reading that Apollo is angry at the Greeks because of him, yet the narrator makes no mention of Agammenon in the introduction. He lays responsiiblity for the many valiant souls swept to Hades at Achilles' feet. This is because Achilles' has the leverage in this situation - the fate of the Greeks is more impacted by his decisions than Agammenons. Agammenon coudl change his mind and if Achilles' chose not to forgive, nothing would happen. Achilles is responsible because he was the greatest of the Greeks in times of war (of course the greatest man in war may be different from the greatest man in peacetime). Hence his companion, not Agammenons, had to die for the Greeks to stand a fighting chance.
		- The singular focus on Achilles from the very beginning suggests that the reader should singularly focus on Achilles throughout the story. But a reader that does so is left wantng for the majority of the epic. For 400+ pages Achilles makes no appearance in the war and barely speaks at all. These middle books detail the combat of the Trojan War, which Achilles is absent from for he has exited the war.
		- Homer describes the Trojan War not from a strategy perspective as a modern one, writing about troop formations, historical battle sites, weapons used, and how the battles played out. He tells the Trojan War by focusing on the actions of the Greek and Trojan princes, warriors of high birth who are foremost on the battlefield. Hence Agammenon is rarely written about besides his role in the feud with Achilles; he is explicitly roasted by the other Greek princes for rarely fighting in the front lines. When he writes about the actions of the warriors however, he tells how they slaughtered individuals, not masses of enemies, by providing detailed accounts of both their kills and biographical details of the killed. Homer tells war as a series of 1 on 1 duels between princes.
		- Historically we know this cannot be true - princes do not seek only princes on the battlefield and avoid all normies. What was Homer's motivation for doing so? Perhaps he saw only the battles between high-born as worthy of writing as it allowed him to emphasize their high-backgrounds and boost the skill and esteem of the general conflict and the warriors who participated in it (and his own fame, as the teling of this conflict).
		- The duels themselves are anticlimactic. More lines are spent on the background of the participants then on the actual fight because one combatant always dies either from the first or second move. Hector runs around the Trojan war three times
		- The story begins with Achilles' boast that he is the greatest of all the Greek warrior. In taking Achilles out of the conflict, the other Greek heroes are forced to step up and are allowed space for indivdiuation and inviting comparison to Achilles. The comparison only closes in the last books when Achilles finally takes the stage and we can just how much greater is his ability to slaughter than the rest of the Greeks. The rest of the Greeks are scared of Hector but Hector, when he sees Achilles, takes flight.
		- Is the Iliad the story of Achilles? Despite the singular focus on him at the beginning because of how big of an impact his absence from the war had (it would have been over very fast if he fought all along), it seems ot be a story of wrath in general. Does wrath motivate the conflict? I wonder what Homer's theory of the soul was. I don't remember Odysseus or Diomedes being motivated by wrath when fighting
