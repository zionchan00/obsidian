	- Intro
		- A natural tension between the philosopher and the rhetor exists and the fact that Athens is ruled by unjust rhetors means the city is not safe for philosophy.  Socrates as a result commands Chaerephon to ask Gorgias ‘who he is’ (447d) at the beginning of the dialogue. Not because he wishes to know Gorgias personally, but because he wishes to know who the rhetor is (447d). This essay argues that the Gorgias should be read as an answer to Socrates’ question so he can best understand how to change the status quo. The dialogue necessarily becomes an exploration of the souls who are attracted to rhetoric as a reaction to this political backdrop and how Socrates can try to change each of them. The advantage of such a reading is that I am able to provide two explanations linking the disparate topics of the dialogue: the nature of rhetoric; whether it is worse to do injustice or suffer injustice; whether it is worse to go punished or unpunished after committing injustice; moderation and hedonism; and the differences and benefits between living the philosophical or political life. Firstly as they relate to the defence of philosophy against rhetoric, and second as an outgrowth of the impact life under tyranny has on the interlocutors' psyche.
	- What is the natural ension between philospher and rhetor
		- The tension between the rhetor and the philosopher begins with their motivations. The rhetors speak to persuade the populace that they are most fit to rule, that their ideas are most aligned with what is good and just (454b), and hence they deserve political power (we can infer that this is what the rhetor desires from 452e ).  When the rhetor spreads truth it is because it is coincidental to their purposes - for their goals they are willing to spread falsehood because their first priority is power.  This becomes more apparent when we analyze the decision-making of a rhetor. The rhetor cares about speed because in practice the situations where rhetors must persuade, such as the decision to go to war or the decision to pass a law, have deadlines which constrain how long the rhetor can spend gathering support. Furthermore the rhetor must also compete against other politicians for the minds of the populace, and the quicker one can persuade a group, the faster they can move onto the next and the more likely it is they can gain support by being the first to persuade someones mind. When the goal is simply to persuade, the rhetor can then adapt what they say to appeal to the listener's existing beliefs such that their views align with the listeners existing worldview (455a). The most persuasive rhetors, and hence the ones most likely to appeal to the populace, will be ones that promise great pleasure to their supporters in exchange for the support, regardless of whether the pleasure desired is good or bad . 
		- Contrast this to the philosopher, who differs in motivation and methodology. Socrates wishes to discover the truth, but we do not see him do so alone. Always he is conversing with someone and in the Phaedrus, he admits he spends all his time in the city, not out in nature, because as a 'lover of learning', 'the country places and the trees are not willing to teach me anything, but the human beings in town are' (230d). Socrates understands that the search for truth is always a collective project, even if only a few of the collective are actively pursuing the truth, because the few are dependent on conversations with the many to begin understanding what was tested to be true and what was assumed blindly. Socrates examines his fellow citizens because before he can escape the cave, he must first know what the cave is and what its limits are. Dialectic is slow however. He has to uncover layers and layers of false assumptions before he can get his interlocutors to acknowledge that they do not know. What better attestation to the difficulty and time-consumption of the project than the winding path the dialogues and his lack of success in many cases? His project would go much faster if his fellow citizens had beliefs which were more accurate, creating an incentive for Socrates to educate his citizens. 
		- The rhetor as Gorgias portrays him is all-powerful. He is able to have the doctors, trainers, and moneymakers as slaves, able to bend all political gatherings to believe what they believe (452e). Furthermore the rhetor has the advantage that beliefs change overtime, meaning the rhetor has a wide set of opinions to conveniently draw from that are highly malleable to the situation at hand. The philosophy poses a threat to the rhetor when he begins raising questions on the truth-value of the rhetors beliefs by challenging their legitimacy by questioning whether the rhetors beliefs really are in alignment with the good and the just. They also spread truth, which is immalleable and poses an annoyance to any political actor (482a-482b. however given Socrates himself swaps our moderation for wisdom as the most important virtue in the Republic, it isn't clear whether he believes Being is unchanging). The rhetor, if Gorgias is not lying, should have the power to persecute the philosopher then because the rhetor will have no problem winning political power, and this is part of the backdrop which the dialogue is set against. It is furthermore not only the rhetor, but even the people under the regime who will become hostile to philosophy overtime. As the Apology demonstrates, people do not enjoy being told they are wrong by a man who will not stop asking questions. The rhetor, who only encourages them in believing they are right, is likely only to worsen their stubbornness. The citizens would then welcome the persecution of Socrates.
