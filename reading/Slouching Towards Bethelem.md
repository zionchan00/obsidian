- The Golden Land and its Golden Dream

	- Some Dreamers of the Golden Dream. Didion never references the golden dream by name after this essay; at most she talks about dreams or desires in the later essays. The placement of all of these essays under the section of the book titled 'The Golden Land and its Golden Dream' lead me to argue that the desires and dreams she talks about are all united, which will become even more apparent by the end of this essay.
	- "This is a story about love and death in the golden land". Stories of love and death are so rare that we can only draw from literature and films for examples of pairings who kill or die because of their other. Yet in the time span of 7 years, Didion was able to list 3 such stories: the murder of Cork Miller by Lucy Miller, the soda pop murders, the murder of Barbara Finch by Bernard Finch. Her essay only discusses the most famous case of these three, the first murder, but her intent is to argue that the quantity of murders and the motivations driving this specific murder are generalizable across the population. The trial dominated the American attention so much that it occupied the front pages of all the major newspapers for the two months it was ongoing, only dethroned by the Academy Awards ceremony. The public was so obsessed that on jury selection day, the courthouse glass shattered from the sheer number of people hurrying to get inside the building. The title of the essay further hints at its generalizability; this is a story of 'some' of the dreamers of the golden dream, there exist many others who desired the things Lucy Miller desired that drove her to murder. In relation to the broader title of the collection, Lifestyles in The Golden Land, perhaps those who dream the Golden Dream come to the Golden Land, or the Golden Land the Golden Dream are the one and the same.
	- In classic Didion style the essay begins with the land. Didion helps the reader place San Benardino Valley on the map by describing it in relation to Los Angeles, ' an hour away'. But the geography is nothing like Los Angeles' 'subtropical twilights' or the 'soft westerlies' of coastal California. Los Angeles is far from paradise but San Benardino sounds like hell - surely the Golden Land isn't here. Breathing is difficult, the wind is devastatingly hot and dry, slicing everything in its path at over a hundred miles an hour, the outdoor filled with the sounds of screams. San Benardino sounds more like a place for those who could not reach the Golden Land.
	- The golden land to Didion is the place where all links to the past are untethered. 'The future always looks good here, because no one remembers the past'. The golden land is a paradise for many because it is a blank slate; it does not prescribe the way forward, it promises that you can become the person you had always wanted to be.
	- But Didion is doubtful that the new personhoods created are authentic. She believes they new lifestyles are mostly derived from newspapers and the movies, and the case of Lucy Miller is a 'tabloid monument' to this new life style.
	- Didion writes that her story is typical of Southern Californian of her time - born somewhere on the prairie and moved here 'in search of something she had seen in the movies or heard on the radio'. Arriving in San Benardino in 1957 with her husband, they try to speed-run the Dream as fast as possible. By 1964 they were getting close: three kids, Christmas tree, family room with the picturesque window, talks of divorce.
	- But by October 7th the family was sixty-four thousand dollars in debt. On that night the couple went on a drive and somehow the car crashed into a wall by the road side, began burning, and Cork Miller, who did not wake up because of the barbiturates he took for sleep, was burned alive. The police investigation was troubling in that materially the case swung both for and against Lucy, so to make a case they needed a strong motive for murder.
	- Social climbing is expensive. Lucy racked up over a thousand-dollars of phone bills in ten months and took on a thirty-thousand dollar mortgage for a larger house. Her husbands death would have paid out over $120,000 in insurance proceeds, so that was one motive. The prosecutors found the other motive in the man who Lucy was having an affair with, a man possessing both the money and people-skills and the good life that Cork seemed to lack.
	- They spoke to one another with words out of bad magazine stories ("Don't kiss me, it will trigger things"). They treated one another like characters out of a James M. Cain novel and the movies of the late 1930s, where violence and threats of blackmail were normalized for the middle class, threatening one another with social ruin by exposing their relationship to the public. As they say, life imitates art - 'the dream was teaching the dreamers how to live'.
	- The detectives were always confused by why this murder case got as much attention as it did. It was not a very interesting murder. What they failed to realize was that the motivations behind the murder, the setting, was what drew the attention of the people
	- The essay ends with Lucy's former lover marrying again a year later. Did he suffer from having his affair exposed over the course of the trial? Didion says maybe he did not - in the golden land where it's believed that the past has no effect on the present or the future, every day the world is born anew.
- If people were learning how to live from popular culture then it became important to analyze the most beloved symbols of Americana at that time. Two later essays, John Wayne: A Love Song and 7000 Romaine, Los Angeles do exactly this.

	- John Wayne: A Love Song tells Didion's own relationship with the legend John Wayne. By relationship I don't mean a romantic one - Didion was able to meet Wayne for this article, but they were purely friends - but a relationship which we now call parasocial. The important thing to keep in mind, like all of these essays, is that the sentiments written here generalize to a broader population.
	- Even though she never grew up be like the heroine's in Wayne's movies, she admits a secret longing to hear her man promise that he will build her a house at the bend in the river where the cotton does not grow, just like Wayne did in War of the Wildcats.
	- Didion characterizes Wayne the actor as a strong-willed, moralistic man, who created a space where agency existed and a man who chose to do right would get the right ending. In contrast to the real-world of uncertainty, vice, and ambiguity, Wayne's world looked even better. He determined 'forever the shape of our dreams'. Wayne is the steadiness which is missing from post-war Americana.
	- 7000 Romaine, Los Angeles is the address of Howard Hughes' business empire. Hughes' was the business man who most occupied the public attention. He owned businesses ranging from airlines to movie studios to missile factories to breweries. He dated the most famous stars of the time like Audrey Hepburn. He was worth $11B at his peak. He set world records flying.
	- He was extraordinary in all of these aspects but Didion's sharp eye landed on another part of his myth. Even though the ostensible role of the media is to report facts, writing on his actions and whereabouts betrayed too much uncertainty to be factual. He appears to, he may be, he might not be, these are the words used to describe his movement in the world.
	- Yet the form of the writing is appropriate for the subject because Hughes' intentions even before he went crazy, was famously secrete and unpredictable. Was the reason he spent 1967 trying to buy up as much of Las Vegas as he could really because the city had restaurants open anytime he wanted a sandwich? Was it true that he paid a barber to be on standby for a haircut at anytime, even testing him by calling him at two in the morning? What sort of man goes missing the last day of negotiations for a loan, leaving men from two of the country' s largest insurance providers and nine of the largest banks waiting, unable to talk to Hughes or his lawyer, only capable of reaching the Lawyer's wife.
	- It's in these anecdotes that Didion finds the secret for why Hughes singularly occupied the publics attention - he was the rawest representation of power used to buy freedom. Hughes could do whatever he wanted and the rest of the world had to bend to his will. Hughes wants to negotiate only at midnight by the municipal dump? It does not matter if you are the president of Convair, if you want Hughes to buy your planes, too bad!
	- It's unlikely for individuals to proclaim their desire to be Hughes publicly because of the obvious anti-social implications. The desire to be Hughes was a revealed, not stated preference.
	- The rise of Hughes as a new type of billionaire over aristocrat philanthropists like Paul Mellon is indicative of a shift in public values. The aristocrat philanthropist, who both believed they had a duty to the people and recognized their unique power as allocator's of capital who were not tied to profit motives, would donate nearly all of their wealth to causes they believed to be good. The only time Hughes ever donated to charity was when, through a complex financial engineering scheme, he donated two of his businesses to two non-profits he had created and loaned them money so that the businesses were tax-exempt and the profits flowed back to him anyways in the form of interest payments.
	- We admire Hughes and Wayne for different reasons. Both displayed the capability to actualize their will on the world, but one was dependable and the other was not. As Didion put out, Hughes was the man you love but not the man you married. Yet in her deep attraction to Wayne, it appears a way out is possible.
- In what way is this desire for power executed? Two different paths are shown in *California Dreaming* essay and *Comrade Laski*
	- The former takes place inside a think tank, the Center for the Study of Political Institutions. When she enters Didion is told not to take a seat at the big table - "the talk there is pretty high-powered". From their descriptions you wold believe that the Center was the think tank that would save democracy; the papers the center released pointed to "the direction for all of us towards a greater understanding". Everything is taped and these tapes, along with pamphlets, are sold to the public and institutions. The Center receives over 75,000 fan letters a year
- Didion's foresight was extraordinary in two ways. Firstly in her understanding that California was the place to go to understand the change that would sweep through the rest of the coastal states, and secondly in her recognition that a mass movement would exist in the first place. She picks the perfect word to describe what's going on - the atomization  of the people.
- The label that comes to mind to describe many of the characters in these stories is narcissistic. The characters interviewed are often transplants from other states, their parents usually separated, and lacking in long-lasting intimate bonds. Combined with their belief that personal relationships are duels where all players have the right to exit at anytime they will so dependency becomes difficult, they experience a loneliness which they try to fill with the attention of the public. These characters also display an overinflated sense of self-importance - this I do not have a good causal explanation for why that is.
