I noted earlier that there is some uncertainty as to whether the wind works for the angels or against them.45 The angels lower their ‘ported Spears’ towards Satan in accordance with the seventeenth-century military command ‘port your pike’. Fowler (like most modern editors) confuses this term with the modern ‘port arms’ and so infers that the angels are not about to attack. Hume (1695) gives us the true picture: ‘With ported Spears; With their Spears born pointed towards him…a military term, to port the Pike’ (166).46 The angels lower their spears from the vertical in readiness to charge (the command ‘port your pike’ immediately preceded ‘charge your pike’), but the same motion that looks intimidating in a bristling phalanx looks feeble when transferred to a field of wheat. Lowering spears threaten to skewer an enemy, but bending cereal stems are in danger of becoming ‘lodged’—a ruined crop, permanently displaced from the vertical. The vehicle of the simile is therefore at odds with the tenor. The angels mean to be aggressive, and their posture (if we see pikes) is aggressive; but the simile implies that they will be beaten down. Far from being ‘an irrelevant notion’, Bentley’s sense of an impending storm pinpoints the problem with this impossible simile. The ploughman is afraid of wind damage and his fears increase proportionally as the angels lower their spears into the attack position.

		- Historical Analysis
			- Ricks: ‘Satan the vulture…has come from the land of the Tartars (suggesting Tartarus)’ (126). Martindale winces at the pun: ‘it is hard to follow Ricks, when he argues that Tartar suggests Tartarus (Milton was an inveterate punster, but this one seems unusually far-fetched)’. The pun may ‘seem’ far-fetched, but it was in fact traditional, having been coined in the thirteenth century by Friar Julian of Hungary, who warned King Béla IV that Europe was about to be invaded by the Tatars or ‘Tartari’, ‘people of hell’.34 (p.376) Matthew Paris, writing soon after the invasion, adopted the pun in his Chronica Majora, where (in his entry for the year 1240) he tells how ‘an immense horde of the detestable race of Satan, the Tartars, burst forth from their mountain-bound regions, and making their way through rocks apparently impenetrable, rushed forth, like demons loosed from Tartarus (so that they are well called Tartars, as it were inhabitants of Tartarus)’.35 The Latin (available to Milton in William Wats’s edition of 1640) reads: ‘exeuntes ad instar daemonum solutorum à tartaro, ut bene Tartari, quasi tartarei, nuncupentur’.36 We know that Milton read Paris, for he cites him often in his prose (in The Tenure he calls him ‘the best of our Historians’).37 Ricks, who relies on his own keen eye and ear, never mentions the tradition underlying the pun, but it exists and it helps his case.

Thus the vulture is like Satan in respect of rapacity, distant knowledge of its prey, and mode of locomotion; while the lambs and yeanling (new-born) kids are like mankind in respect of innocence, and division into redeemed sheep and reprobate goats (Matt. XXV). One journey is from snowy Imaus, which notably failed to bound the roving Tartar Genghis Khan, to the rivers of India; the other from the ‘frozen continent’ (ii 587) of Tartarus, which did not keep Satan from roving, to Eden with its rivers.…More subtly, the barren plains of Sericana correspond to the primum mobile not merely because both are stopping places, but also because in both the elements are confused. (The Chinese use sails, the means of propulsion for ships, on their vehicles; and the primum mobile is a sea of land.) This emphasis on confusion of the elements perhaps implies that Satan’s entry into our world is an inroad of chaos. (III. 431–41n)
