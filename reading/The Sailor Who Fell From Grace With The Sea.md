- Review
	- When one is told that they are maturing or becoming an adult, it's meant to be interpreted as a compliment. A closer examination of the goalpost turns the compliment into an unintended roast.The older I get, the less alive people in my age group seem to be. In spirit I am on the set of The Walking Dead - a conversation on any university campus about how people choose their careers and one admires how little consideration for one’s own nature there is, how selfless the answers are, how little excitement there is for life.
	  Unironically I would be much happier if someone called me childlike. Listen to the excitement in the voices of children when they learn something new and run over quickly to tell you! See how wide their eyes open when they gain a new frame to understand the world with! The joy that emanates through their being to be alive, to walk the world! Admire how pure their motivations are, no care for 'this is good for your career', you 'SHOULD' do this, you 'HAVE' to do this; no, all they are concerned with is whether the activity compliments their nature - are they having fun? Most mature humans seem incapable of asking themselves such a simple question, yet to a child it is all they know. How anyone can examine such change and call it 'growth' is laughable to me.
	  This is the wonder which is alighted within some of us at birth and, for an even smaller population, burns throughout their whole life. The process of ‘maturing’ and ‘growing up’ is mainly a smothering of this flame by the defeat of idealism at the hands of an evil that masquerades as ‘responsibility’, ‘practicality’, and other conventions..

	  It is harder for the lust for life to burn in our time than ever before. It makes less ‘practical’ sense to take risks, to long for the ideal when the conventional is so comfortable, so nice. The few serious causes which remain have deteriorated from neglect- our nations, our communities, our work. Our increased interconnectivity makes us more aware than ever of how banal most lives are and the sense that there is not much to be done anymore, either because all that can be possibly discovered has been found or that inhuman mass systems block our way. No more are there continents to be mapped; space came and left the public imagination, the deep sea fails to provoke the same sense of wonder and amazement. Every model globe found in the grade school classroom seems to affirm that there is nothing left to explore.
	  This is the struggle Mishima's characters deal with in The Sailor Who Fell From Grace With The Sea. Ryuji, who gives up the ideal out of ‘practicality’ but still longs for it. Fusako, who believes she caught a small fragment of the dreamer in Ryuji but is unable to bring it out. Noboru and his gang, who are aware that adulthood tilts the battlefield in the favor of the enemy but have yet to find a higher ideal to dedicate their lives to. The best they can do is to dream of becoming commercial seafarers - pah, not even pirates.
	- While I applaud the mission and his prose is beautiful, Mishima struggles to capture exactly what is missing and longed for by his characters. Both he and his characters are aware of the problem of communicating what seems to be near incommunicable, as is the problem with any divine sensation. Ryuji's disappointment in his inability to communicate these feelings to Fusako and the urge he feels to tone down his stories and resort to small-parent talk with Noboru; Fusako's disappointment when she expected Ryuji to speak a passionate declaration or a profound monologue, but gets a pop song instead; the incoherent speeches of the chief concerning what exactly he hates about the world and what he wishes to accomplish. The closest Noboru gets to what he has been looking for up until then is found not through speech, but the visceral overloading of his bodily senses from the scream of the ship’s horn in tandem with Fusako and Ryuju’s passionate consummation. It is in this overwhelming experience that reverberates through all of Noboru's being that he realizes, yes, what he longs for really exists! Beauty and wonder are so great that speech always falters in comparison to the experience. I hope many of us can one day experience such a eureka moment, a turning around.
	- Fear not adventurers, there is no need to kill others (or yourselves). There are still great causes, hard problems, and worthy struggles to dedicate ourselves to; the death of the romantic, the idealist, has been greatly exaggerated. This book may not be the rallying cry of the movement, but it is beautiful.

An even smaller portion of these children will retain their sense of adventure as they age.

Noboru and his gang, who are aware that these feelings fade away with adulthood, and why they find the sea and its ships so attractive - to them, the sea is the only place of escape left. He feels robbed and betrayed that the one source of inspiration for him and status in the eyes of his peers no longer can be.
	- One counter-argument is that this time period is a consequence of innocence, how little the young know about the world and the sheltering of the young from the practical demands of the world. The first is false because it misunderstands the driver of the above behaviour, wonder, which is not sated when fed but grows instead. Every discovery brings you such joy that you wish to make more discoveries, the more discoveries you make the more you realize how many more pleasures await you.  criticism were true, is the world by nature so terrible that the more one knows, the more one wishes to know less of it and be shielded from its being? Or has it been shaped by the humans walk its ground such that when a child discovers the world of adults, their horizons do not expand, they narrow. The second is more on the mark.
