	- Every speaker in the dialogue offers us a part of Eros, and it is the work of the reader to put them together to attempt forming a whole. Any human can relate to the increased weight of opinion that our lovers have as the beings closest to our hearts which Phaedrus speaks about. To him love is always a good because we wish to avoid committing shameful deeds in the face of our loved ones and instead desire to act virtuously so their loved ones love them more. His account of Eros is flawed in a a few ways. Firstly he fails to consider the possibility that one's love has a twisted sense of virtue and shame, and hence one becomes worse, not better. If his account were true, virtue would be an easy thing, for most people fall in love and hence most people would be virtuous, but we know this not to be the case. Secondly he fails to give an argument on why love is the best mechanism instead of say, philosophy, which provides knowledge of the shameful and beautiful. Some open questions I have are: what makes an act shameful or great and beautiful? Lovers dying for their lovers may be praiseworthy in the eyes of the Gods, but what exactly is Phaedrus' reasons for why they do so (i.e is it bc to not do so is shameful)?
	- Pausinias adds onto Phaedrus' speech in arguing for the possibility that Eros can be base. He differentiates between an Eros Pandemus, an Eros which falls in love with anyone and is makes the act just as likely to be good as to be bad, and Eros Urania, an Eros which selects for those who are sensible and hence is praiseworthy. For Pausinias the development of eros is directly related to the development of logos, reason/speech. In countries such as Boetia where no differentiation is made between Eros Pandemus and Eros Urania, the people are 'incapable of speaking' (182b). In countries such as Ionia and the barbarian states where any gratification of lovers is shamed along with 'pederasty as well as philosophy and the love of gymnastics', it is because they wish to prevent the birth of 'great and proud thoughts be engendered among their subjects, any more than strong friendships and associations' (182c). He contrasts this to Athens, where Eros Urania is praised and whenever it possesses a lover, the pursued beloved is not shamed for accepting that lover. The status of love in Athens is so high that lovers are allowed to "perform acts of slavishness that not even a slave would" (183c), acts which if they were done for money or political power, great shame would fall upon them. "For neither money nor political favors are though to be stable or lasting, to say nothing of the fact that in the natural course of things, no noble and generous friendship comes out of them" (184c). Through the comparison of the states, it appears Eros Urania gives birth to our ability to reason, and hence philosophy, which could explain the particularly philosophical character that Athens has. In shaming money and political power for love on the basis that the former two are short-term, Pausinias implies that to desire that which is permanent and lasting is praiseworthy, and hence he draws a connection between Eros and permanence which Diotima later expands on in Socrates' speech. Pausinias also expands our notion of Eros from the purely romantic love that Phaedrus talked about to love of friends, love of metaphysical beings, and friendships. Some open questions I have are why his speech places the onus of noble/base purely on the beloved with no talk about the lover. Is he implying that lovers have no power over who they fall in love with and hence whether they are virtuous or not, so education should focus on the beloveds raising their standards on who they accept/reject? How can lovers begin to be virtuous and the beloved select for virtuous lovers if they don't know what virtue is? What even is virtue?
	- Eryximachus has the most peculiar speech, praising Eros because it resides not only in the soul of humans but also in beings everywhere. To him the art of medicine is the art of gratifying the healthy over the unhealthy desires of the body. The two Eros' are 'different and dissimilar with one another', desiring different and similar things, and the good doctor is "able to make the things that are most at enmity in the body into friends and to make them love one another", creating agreement from difference (186e). Harmony in music could also be viewed as Eros he argues; music composed of different notes of different positions can have a most agreeable sound. But things that are different cannot agree while they are different - they must come together in 'mutual love and unanimity'. So the art of music is the creation of agreement from prior difference, the same as the art of medicine. He sees lyric poetry and education as tools to gratify the decent human being, "as well as those who are not as yet decent, so that they might become more decent" (187e). Just like how eating unhealthy foods is alright in small quantities to harvest the great pleasure that comes from within, satisfying Eros Pandemus is alright in his view to experience those pleasures, but one must stop before intemperance grows. Eryximachus also sees the Eros' in the seasons; hot vs cold, dry vs moist, and balance must be achieved between them, a 'moderate mixture' so the season is good.
	- What is confusing about his speech however is the change in how he believes the soul of the human should be treated. He begins his speech by stating that Eros Pandemos must not be gratified but as he dives into music, where the good is produced by the two notes agreeing with one another, and the seasons, where moderation between hot and cold is needed to produce good weather, he concludes that the same moderation between the two Eros' must happen in the human body. While he is correct in seeing the commonality of dual natures across many beings, the mistake he makes is that the relationship between hot and cold is different from Eros Urania and Eros Pandemus in that in the latter relationship one is objectively more preferable, more noble, than the other. Perhaps this is not a mistake however and what he wished to suggest the possibility that gratifying Eros Pandemus is not always harmful, and it is possible to both have your cake and eat it the same way one treats sweets. Eryximachus adds to the discussion by abstracting Eros to simply dual natures, extending where we think Eros resides, but also linking the idea of unanimity to Eros, the desire to be one.
	- The myth Aristophanes  tells in his speech is an explanation of why Eros can be felt as a desire for that which we long for to become a part of us, why we pull that which we love deep within us. In the myth humans used to be double in all organs and of three races; one with male/female organs, another female/female, another male/male. After humanity tried to dethrone the Olympians, they were punished by Zeus cutting them in half, and hence Eros is the desire for our other half. "Love is the desire and pursuit of the whole" (193a). Eros is to be praised as "the bringer-together of ... ancient nature, who tries to make one out of two and to heal ... human nature" for when we are united again, the greatest happiness will be felt. Like Pausinias, he believes the greatest version of Eros is the one in which males seek the companionship of other men, even when they are married to a woman and have a family, for they must still obey law. He describes their desire for one another to not be sexual but to be of such force that if Hephaestus asked if they would like to be joined together, they would immediately agree. What Aristophanes has described is friendship between the best of friends. It seems Eros is not just a romantic, sexual longing, but also a longing for companions who complete us, who we deeply love. Most interestingly Aristophanes' account completely lacks a distinction between the two Eros'; he praises the god unqualifiedly. As long as one finds the other who completes him, even if one is unvirtuous and hence the other is unvirtuous, happiness is created and hence the act is praiseworthy. Perhaps he does not make the distinction because his Eros belongs to Eros Pandemus, one of those who "have an eye only to the act and are unconcerned with whether it is noble or not" (181c), as Pausinias says. Aristophanes captures the idea of finding our missing half, the one who completes us, who once we meet we feel as if they should have been there the whole time.

	- Socrates, the speech which all must have been waiting for, begins first by roasting the speech of Agathon for attributing any and all good things to Eros and not telling the truth. He too will praise Eros, but only to the extent that the praise is true. He establishes firstly that what we desire we do not have at present. This is true even of a man who wishes to be tall despite being tall right now; he wishes to be tall in the future which is not at present, and hence he wishes for that which is missing from his being right now. Hence if Eros desires the beautiful and the good, Eros cannot be beautiful and good, so Agathon is incorrect to say he is happy. Socrates' speech becomes a retelling of his education from his teacher of erotics, Diotima. She teaches him that just because Eros is not beautiful does not mean he is ugly. Rather is in between the beautiful and the ugly, similar to how correct opinion is in between knowledge and ignorance of anything at all. For Eros' parents are Poverty and Resource; from Poverty he is lack while Resource makes him desirous to fill the lack. Tragically "that which is supplied to him is always flowing out" (203e), leaving him forever wanting. In being between knowledge and understanding he is like the philosopher, who knows just enough to know that he wishes to know more, but not enough to say they possess wisdom. If the gods are beautiful and happy and good, Eros cannot be a god, but he is also not man. He is inbetween, a daemonic being, who bridges communication between the two by passing on requests from humans to gods and orders from gods to humans. The daemonic man is one who is possessed by the daemon, in between knowledge and ignorance, Resource and Poverty.
	- Diotima offers an alternate explaination of why Eros desires what it does. Eros desires both the beautiful and the good but Socrates can only answer why it desires the good (happiness) and not the beautiful, raising the possibility that attaining the beautiful will not bring happiness. From Agathon we know the good to be beautiful but it is unclear whether all beautiful things are good. Happiness is the ultimate end; one cannot ask why we desire happiness or what we desire happiness for, the chan of questions ends here. Eros is therefore the desire which pulls us towards the peaks of life. Every human being is guided by Eros but the reason we fail to realize this is that one of the paths to happiness was given the name of the all of the paths to happiness - the path being loving as we call it. Diotima claims in reality money-making, philosophy, gymnastics are all the longing for the good so we can be happy. But Socrates is sceptical, responding 'what you say is probably true'. Perhaps he believes money-making seems to be a path to happiness but is not so in actuality.
	- After this analysis Diotima restates what eros is: "eros is the whole desire of good things and being happy" (205d) and because one would always wish to have the good things and be happy, "eros is of the good's being one's own always" (206a). Notice that the beautiful is not mentioned. I infer her stance towards the beautiful to be the same as the half or the whole as laid out in 205e. In this passage she explicitly rejects Aristophane's eros in which eros is the desire for completion unless the completion is good, for humans will amputate themselves if they deem it good. With this statement she completes Aristophanes' analysis by accounting for the possibility that one's other half could be a negative influence and detrimental to one's happiness.
	- This is when the dialogue makes the biggest conceptual jump. Diotima asks Socrates what do those who we say are possessed by eros do to get the good, what deed? Earlier she listed philosophizing, money-making, gymnastics as ways of loving, Eros in action, so they too shall fall under the category she is about to describe. Socrates, unable to answer, defers to Diotima for the answer, and she replies that 'their deed is bringing to birth in beauty both in terms of the body and in terms of the soul' (206b). She explains that all human beings become pregnant in either body or soul at a certain time in their age. If they come close to the ugly their pregnancy will terminate, but close contact to the beautiful will instead lead to a succesful birth. Linking this back to the earlier discussion on philosophizing as Eros, Diotima may explain it as coming close to the beautiful to give birth to knowledge which makes one happy. Eros is commonly believed to be of the beautiful in that one desires the beautiful to get the things within them out, but the true end of eros is the good and happiness. Eros is 'of engendering and bringing to birth in the beautiful' (206e).
	- Diotima further argues that Eros 'be of immortality too' (207a) because Eros was earlier agreed to be the desire of the good to always be one's own, and hence Eros is is the desire for the immortal good. She asks Socrates what the cause of this eros and desire is and then proceeds to not name what the cause is at all. She instead gives an example of Eros in action in animals, who will fight stronger animals for the sake of their young and starve so their young can weak. She reasons they do so because 'mortal nature seeks as far as possible to be forever and immortal' (207d). By creating, what is dying can be replaced and a stable state achieved, the closest a mortal can get to immortality. She believes human beings do the same and to prove this she appropriates Phaedrus' examples of the Queen who died for her King and Achilles dying to avenge Patroclus to prove this. Phaedrus reasoned they did so because their loved ones meant that much to them but Diotima argues it is only because of the immortal honour which they knew they were bound to receive that led them to do so. "I believe that all do all things for the sake of immortal virtue and a famous reputation of that sort; and the better they are, so much the more is it thus; for they love the immortal" (208e).
	- Some are pregnant in the bodies and have kids to try to be immortal and happy. Others become pregnant with virtue in the soul thanks to the poets and other craftsmen and look towards the beautiful to give birth. To Diotima the greatest part of Prudence is 'is the arranging and ordering of the affairs of cities and households. Its name is moderation and justice' (209b). Those who are pregnant in the soul will become politicians to improve their cities. To do so they must search for beauty first in a body, before realizing beauty exists in all bodies, so they must move onto beauty in the soul, then the beauty in the sciences, and lastly they will discovery beauty itself. The form of beauty which is perfectly itself will create the most perfect virtue inside the soul of the lover.
	- That concludes Socrates speech and a drunken Alcibiades comes crashing into the scene. It appears he has a quarrel with Socrates because Socrates spurned his advances. Alcibiades agrees to give a speech in which he "shall assault the man (Socrates) and take vengeance" (213e) by telling the truth and "praise Socrates, through likeness" to two creatures of myth (215a). The first is Silenus in 215b, a woodland god who  was said to be very wise but once caught, only said that it was better not to be born. Hallow statues of Silenus are made in which "images of gods within" can be found, and it is these statues Alcibiades likens Socrates to. The second simile is Marsyas, who's flute was able to charm any human being, except Socrates replaces the flute with speeches. His speeches charm Alcibiades to the effect that his soul grew troubled and he 'distressed at my slavish condition" (215e). After being exposed to Socrates' speeches many more times, he could only conclude that "it was not worth living in the way I am" (216a). Alcibiades explicitly says the simile is not only physical. He believes Socrates has an outward shell which is far different from his inner being. Outwardly he appears to be 'erotically inclined to the beauties and is always around them', yet inwardly he is 'full he is of moderation', 'not at all concerned if someone is beautiful seeing it in the same light as wealth or any other honour which the many praise'. Outwardly he claims to be 'ignorant of everything and knows nothing' (216d-e), inwardly if one examines his speeches as they do to silenuses, 'they alone of speeches have sense inside; and, second, that they are most divine and have the largest number of images of virtue in them' (222a). In reality Socrates 'believes that all these possessions are worth nothing and that we are nothing ... and all his life he keeps on being ironical and playful to human beings'.  Only one look into Socrates' insides, the esoteric truth, for Alcibiades to be so mesmerized that he 'had to do just about whatever Socrates commanded' (217a).
	- Alcibiades exemplifies Phaedrus' eros when he say only Socrates can make him feel shame for he knows Socrates is always right in what he criticizes Alcibiades for neglecting himself to look after the city instead because he has "succumbed to the honour I get from the many" (216b). There is tension here between two accounts of Eros - Phaedrus' and Diotima's, and the tension gives Alcibiades' Eros a political twist. In attempting to convince Alcibiades to abandon his duties to the polis to fulfill his duties to his own soul, Socrates is putting the city in danger by charming its talented youth to stop servicing it and spend their time philosophizing instead. He is doing exactly what the city accused of him in the Apology of Socrates. What keeps Alcibiades loyal to the city is Eros as described by Diotima, the longing for immortality. Our longing for immortality keeps man political. Being charmed by Socrates, Alcibiades has fallen in love with a high soul who makes him feel shame for wanting to be great in the eyes of the city. Who said immortal honor was necessarily a good? Even Diotima did not make such a claim. Inward philosophizing is not an act which rewards one with immortal virtue. The most famous philosopher, Socrates, became famous by virtue of indulging in his political nature, wandering around the city talking to others and becoming famous for his way of questioning. For this part of Eros to be fulfilled there must be others to know of your greatness. I feel the same way when I read a great work, the temptation to dive into the book and its mysteries, dive entirely within and forget the world outside. What keeps me outside is the desire for recognition, the desire for action, to know the impact one's actions have on the world, to assert meaning onto its face.
