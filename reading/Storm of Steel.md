- Interpretive Essay
	- "We had left lecture-room, class-room, and bench behind us. We had been welded by a few weeks' training into one corporate mass inspired by the enthusiasm of one thought … to carry forward the German ideals of '70. We had grown up in a material age, and in each one of us there was the yearning for great experience, such as we had never known. The war had entered into us like wine. We had set out in a rain of flowers to seek the death of heroes. The war was our dream of greatness, power, and glory. It was a man's work, a duel on fields whose flowers would be stained with blood. There is no lovelier death in the world … anything rather than stay at at home, anything to  make one with the rest .." pg1
	- "The horrible was undoubtedly a part of that irresistible attraction that drew us into the war. A long period of law and order, such as our generation had behind it, produces a real craving for the abnormal, a craving that literature stimulates" pg 23
	- The 'generation' which Junger describes above is a transhistorical concept; I too belong to such a generation. I am uncertain where most of my peers steer their desire for the abnormal but I know for sure that most don't do what Junger did - run away from home to join the French Foreign Legion at the age of 18. This difference in action reveals the difference in beliefs between us: where Junger views war as a good, a solution to the emptiness of a safe life,  most in my generation would deny war any good at all. Where Carl Schmitt tells us that war is inexorable,  *Storm of Steel* tells us that war is desirable. It is this difference in values that makes Junger worth reading, for Junger's experiences push us to rethink the conventional view of what the good life is.
	- What Junger finds desirable about war is his belief that only on the battlefield, in staking our lives for an ideal which is greater than the life of any individual or even the lives of an entire people, that the most worthy ideals, which are always abstract, become concrete. Most of us feel some belief that we love our country and would fight for it if demanded. Without a concrete situation where our bond with our ideals is not only tested but strengthened, we can only intellect a shallow part of these principles. On the battlefield where you and millions of others risk their lives for their homeland, the homeland is not understood, but *deeply felt in your heart*. The gains from war are not a rational understanding of an ideal but an experience of the ideal in action, an experience which Junger loves.
	- Junger only learned the above after his time in the first world war. We must then ask what preconception of war did he have such that at my age, he ran away from home to join a foreign army to experience it, while my preconceptions of it do not create the same desire. The difference is that Junger belonged to a time where war could be romanticized, when people could still believe in the gentleman's war. Gentleman are to understand war as an objective matter where your personal feelings should not be at play.

	- The desire of both the gentleman and their enemy to kill one another is so strong that it breaks the impulses against taking the lives of others which society breeds within us to prevent violence. A true gentleman is able to both let the desire consume him and also restrain himself as soon as the opponent surrenders out of respect for them. But if the goal of war is to win and to win requires one to commit acts which are not respectable, it is only a matter of time before one side employs such strategies and the other side chooses to also break the rules or face defeat. How could they not break the rules when the lives of millions and the very existence of their homeland is at stake? The gentleman respects the enemy for their fighting skill but the greater their skill and the more valiantly they fight, the more of the gentleman's brothers in arms die. After someone has killed your brothers, how could you restrain yourself from killing them? Which will win - the gentleman's respect or the killer's bloodlust?
	- The contradictions of the gentleman's war are not found in literature but in reality, which is why Junger is disappointed with war in actuality. He witnesses his peers lose control of themselves to their bloodlust, killing enemies even after they surrender. Further adding to his disappointment is the increasing involvement of technology on the battlefield. It was now possible to fight a whole battle, kill thousands of the enemy, without ever seeing the enemy. The battlefield used to be filled with the cheers and cries of thousands clashing swords, fighting hand to hand; now it was filled with the metal clang of artillery pieces launching their explosives, each shell able to tear an entire squad to slivers with shrapnel. On the modern battlefield, the ease and quickness with which human dies, the distance between the killer and the killed, seems to have lowered the value of human life and decreased the empathy one has with the enemy, supporting the urge to be an animal and destroy, destroy, destroy.
	- "The very laws of nature seemed to have lost their validity. The air shimmered as though on a day of summer heat. The changing index of refraction made fixed objects dance to and fro. Black streaks of shadow flitted across the mass of smoke. The rear had become a norm and one heard no longer. One could scarcely hear the thousands of machine-guns in our rear that swept the blue sky with swarm upon swarm of lead" pg. 251
	- The problem is Junger's inability to see the contradictions of the gentleman's war created by the opposition between the desire to win the war and the desire to win the war in a specific manner which makes him blind to the inevitable death of the concept. Most men are not gentlemen like Junger - precisely because it is so difficult to be a gentleman, to resist falling, that Junger admires those who pass the test. The many fall victim to bloodlust, and push conflict towards ever escalating levels of destruction. Every death makes it harder and harder to resist bloodlust; if an atrocity is committed against your people, your respect for the enemy is diminished and the desire to kill them grows. The desire to win the war, to survive at all costs, the animal instinct, stands triumphant over the human. When his peers murder the surrendered, he says it cannot be helped. "Privation and danger tear away all that has been acquired, and then good form survives only in those in whom it is born" pg 192. Junger's experience proves that few have good form within, so we are fortunate that we live in a time where their true nature is not revealed.
	- So even without technology, it is questionable whether the Jungerian ideal of 'objective war' really ever existed in reality. War threatens that which our animal instincts always push us to value the most, our lives. Junger is a man from the past, a man who believed how one lived and died was less important than the duration of life. The problem is in a world where all men hold these ideals, it is advantageous in war to break those ideals and pursue victory no matter the means, wiping out the idealists.
	- "What do you do when there is an evil you cannot defeat by just means? Do you stain your hands with evil to destroy evil? Or do you remain steadfastly just and righteous even if it means surrendering to evil?" - Lelouch Vi Britannia
	- Without his ideals, our long arc of history has created a race towards annihilation, humans constantly seekng to outdo one another in ways to win wars, to destroy the enemy.  Junger is correct that war, with all its animality, also provides the greatest tests of humanity. War can push ordinary-looking men towards superhuman acts of heroism, push people across class and race to come together through sacrifices in living standards and a love for their homeland, push scientists to work harder and better to advance the technology of the homeland, and push destroyers of lives to show kindness, empathy, and forgiveness to the enemy and the other. But we can no longer look towards war for this purpose.
