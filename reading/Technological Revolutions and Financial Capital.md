- How Financial capital drives technological revolutions
	- At the beginning of a tech revolution, there needs to be excess financial capital which is so dissatisfied with existing rates of return from the old paradigm that it looks for risky, uncertain opportunities - the next paradigm
		- the owners of this excess financial capital are either incumbent firms from the last revolution, the kings, who are seeing declining rates of growth because they have fully maxxed out their existing markets and are looking for new markets to penetrate. Either that, or give their public shareholders back their money or buyback your own stock to boost it. In theory there are financial incentives for you to do whatever is highest ROI, even if it means giving money back to investors and saying there isn't really anything for you to do, because this will lead to a higher stock price and that makes you richer and your employees happier. But there are also personal reasons, like career advancement, that can push someone to want to do otherwise as i explore in [[Theory of Economy]]. Ego, career advancement, can push peopel to want a company to grow
			- What do public shareholders do with the money? What is the structure of a modern hedge fund? Likely that money is recycled into further public market opportunities - the fund size grows further. So the money doesn't get recycled back into the early-stage innovation so to speak,
		- the second source of capital is actually the investor base. LPs will have accumulated tons of profit, most of which they'll be expected to keep investing becaue budgets rarely ever require all of that money to be spent, whether you're a charity or an endowment or a HNW. These LPs will have gotten rich off of investing in the previous tech revolution, so they're looking for places where they can spend that money. The thing is LOTS of money is floating around now (where is this money beign reallocated away from? other sectors, I should checkout the outflows of money from non-tech sectors into tech to get a better picture of this). when they look at their set of opportunities, they'll be pushed further out of the risk curve because so many other investors have tons of money
	- As a consequence we see risky assets broadly get funded, including new technologies, and for the next revolution to take place some of them have to see some traction. At the sign of their popularity we see more and more funding head towards these areas, as more people see signs that these technologies have massive profit-generating potential. As a bonus, we should also see capital flood towards emerging markets and risky assets. 
	- A false peak happens. People get very rich, we see a new economy pop up, and everyone predicts the future has arrived. Those who are part of the new economy, whether as employees or owners or investors, get wealthy, and they are a really small % of the population given how new everything is. But usually the extent of the demand for these new technologies and the rate of improvement in their capabilities is overestimated, and we see a crash in their valuations. The gap between supply and demand has grown even larger, and what happens now is that the quantity of products from this new revolution are cheap, allowing for further experimentation from entrepreneurs with the technology. This is able to counteract the decline in financing, and founder slowly begin to build companies that are much more disciplined in their growth
	- more and more success stories overtime, and eventually there is recognition that a new technological revolution is upon us and a new way of doing business exists. These new incumbents become the superstars of the economy, and are rewarded with rich valuations in both public and private markets because there are high expectations for continued growth going into the future. Moreover an entire ecosystem of investable opportunities exist, as a new value chain is being created, giving venture capital investors even more of a reason to be optimistic and hyper-focus on the stack, as there is more money to be made and a real first-mover advantage exists.
	- Moreover we'll also see the new technology diffuse to the rest of the economy, both because older companies see it as a tool to boost profitability but also as new incumbents search for new ways to generate revenue once they begin maxxing out their markets. The new technology will also diffuse to the rest of the world, which leads to 
		- investors investing in emerging markets on the basis of the belief that this new technology can increase rates of profit and also because they have a knowledge advantage from witnessing the unfolding of the paradigm already in a more advanced economy. There's a funny historical pattern of Brazil's economy seeing a wave of foreign capital at the false and real peaks of every past technological revolution

- New Distortions: Modern Venture Capital and Software
	- Historical move for investors: buy new infra and hold, sell before short-term peak falls, and then watch the app-layer over the long-run. https://cdixon.org/2013/02/10/the-computing-deployment-phase/ is a good example of thi slogic
	- The modern twist to this understanding of technological revolutions is the dramatic increase in the size of the venture capital industry in comparison to the past, which is why Perez's theory needs an update.
	- Venture capital as defined by the funding of new companies with next-to-no funding has existed always throughout history - early IPO's during the creation of the stock market, many inventors throughout history raised money, and even more recently in the '60s the traitorous six also raised money from investors. 
	- There is a difference between the modern venture capital industry and these early investors however. Firstly is the creation of a 'factory' model of funding, and secondly is the hindsight of history. Firstly, modern venture capital has grown to a large enough size that investors have begun specializing into different groups, each focusing on investing at a different stage of company maturity. Secondly, investors have grown up in a time of rapid technological change and have sufficient history to study to try to form theories of technological change. 
		- The implication of the factory model of investment is that a firm can sell to a better product to LPs, because they could diversify their portfolio better
	- The implication for both of these for Perez' s theory is that an increasingly large investor class, many of which have studied Perez, will distort her theory and hence it is important to understand in what ways her theory will fail to match onto the next world. Moreover, I argue that technological revolutions need to be further distinguished by the degree to which confusion can exist at the 'app-layer', which would lengthen the quantity of time it takes for everyone to figure out the 'new normal' and for the revolution to fully settle in.
	- Firstly I'll explain the distinciton I make between the infrastructure layer and the application layer. Infrastructure is sold to other businesses, who use it to create products which are sold to civilizans. Nowadays the product layer would be called the 'application-layer' because technology is mostly associated with software. 
	- What is noteworthy about the application layer is that truly new technological revolutions are so expansive in capability that it takes years before entrepreneurs figure out new use cases which best exploit these new possibilities. The classic example is Uber, Instacart, etc, many of the darlings of the 2010 startup era took a decade after the dotcom crash to emerge. Historically, this looks like .... and .... from previosu books (i wonder what the disruptive use cases were for the railroad era, steam era, etc). 
	- Investors have therefore learned to identify the pattern of early lack of product-market fit in verticals not as a sign that an industry lacks product market fit, but rather that this is industry is a new technological revolution in the making, and hence we should invest into the sector. The only problem so far is that entrepreneurs have yet to identify the correct 'application-level' use cases. 
	- The problem as an investor is that it's difficult to ascertain which app-layer bet will payoff in the earlier stages of a technological revolution so if one is optimistic on a sector, it makes sense to invest at the infrastructure layer, which is usually agnostic to any one particular use case. Investors will then begin to hype up the app layer to generate revenue for the infra layer, and try their best to spur the development of an app ecosystem. If things go well, perhaps this is the boom and entrepreneurs will figrue it out now. Worst case, they figure it out later. 
	- The problem however is that the revenue of infrastructure players is ultimately dependent on the revenue of the application layer, and if there truly is a 'mismatch' as Perez's theory indicates, from people failing to figure out the correct use cases for the app layer, then we should expect to see falling revenue for infra players too.
