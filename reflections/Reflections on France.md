- When one walks around the city the first thing they notice is the sheer number of food-related places to eat there are. Every 4th commercial space on the first floor of buildings had to have been food-related. In France there are bistros, boulangeries (bakeries), brasseries (pubs to drink and eat at), bouillons (french food for cheap, these were the largest French restaurants in terms of space I found) and caf's (coffee shops).
- I believe there are a few factors at play which create such a large density of food-related places in Paris. On the demand side, I felt a larger desire for eating out in place of cooking in Paris than I do in Toronto. There are many in my age group who cannot cook and are dependent on food delivery companies as a result but I get the sense that Parisians are not the biggest fans of food delivery. To me one of the defining traits of Parisian culture is slow-living, the belief that life's activities are best enjoyed by long hours of time spent. I would guess that this behavior came from a childhood steeped in the deep cultural heritage which the French are the heir of. Imagine from a young age being taught how to appreciate the different components that make up an art piece; what effect the shade of colors has on the image, the lines, the subject matter, the display (or lack) of depth, etc. Being trained with such a mental model of the visual arts would open Parisians to the incredible detail that exists within each work of art, pushing them to walk slower through the museum. Contrast this to the archetypal tourist, who speedwalks through the museum and can only nod 'interesting' to every art piece.
- The attitude of slow-living is reflected in the long eating times of the Parisians. Lunch breaks are two hours here and people can sit on the dinner table at 7 and still not be gone by 11. Combined with a French tendency to be late, lines can form outside of French restaurants from reserved tables being occupied over the time they were booked for, but the owners are unwilling to kick the diners out even after they have finished their meal. These factors along with the smaller square footage of restaurants restricts the supply of available seating, further encouraging a larger number of restaurants.
