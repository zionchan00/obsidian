There are a few investors and firms I find remarkable, and I wanted to write out what I've learned form studiyng their evolution and. 


  
I think the most remarkable thing about the sequoia partnership is how long term thinking they are about their own positioning as a business. Every new fund strategy except China (growth, public, India) they launched endured terrible returns for the first 5 years. What mistake was made was hiring an incumbent every single time who wasn’t used to the new way of investing, seeing most of them fail (or they literally mutiny and leave like sequoia India did), but then finding the gem of an employee who showed promise and promoting them to lead. The stewards continued to play an active role in nudging the firm even after having retired (Moritz was the one who made them raise the $8B growth fund after the $100B Softbank). And when the fiims track record was in danger after the dot com crash, the partners stepped in to pour in their own personal money to backstop LP's and ensure that they made money. That willingness to net just ' give up profits', but to actively bet their own net worth in order to do what is right for the people that supported the firm - that showed how much pride and responsibility they felt towards the Sequioa brand.

  
And the TBD on the newest generation of firms; Greenoaks and Thrive. 

  
It’s no coincidence that the two biggest firms that matter right now in investing are running the exact same strategy both in perception and in fund size. They invest across the stages but the majority of their returns come at the growth-stage. They both threw 50% of their fund into a single company early on in their history, and have later invested a quarter of their funds (OpenAI, stripe, databricks). Both of these funds run very lean teams (<15 investment professionals) and face no restrictions in geography, stage, or sector. In doing so, they actually end up somewhat matching the end structure of Sequoia at the end of the Power Law: global, stage agnostic, vertical agnostic, just long technology in every way.  

  
They both have strong cults of personality around their founder and make it a big deal for their founders to be involved (Neil does the first meeting for every single GO investment).

  
‘The firms that win deals are the ones that want them the most’. This comes from being scrappy and hungry, willing to fly to a war zone to close a deal if needed. But you also need the conviction to know this is the right thing to do, after all you can’t be throwing yourself so hard at every single opportunity. 

Paradoxically, ‘the more ambitious the company, the less competition there is’ is a line J think a lot about from Kareem. Because no one else is willing to pay the prices you are because they don’t believe in the visions possibility or see the potential. This is why both of them have been able to invest in times of distress at cheap prices (Carvana for both firms, Greenoaks also did Rippling during SVB, Thrive was the only term sheet pre-chat gpt launch for OpenAI) but even during normal raises (the latest OAI, DB, rounds from thrive, and stripe during COVID). Everybody saw these companies, few moved with the size that they did. 

Trust is important. They’ve made themselves first calls for founders who need large checks in times of distress. Sam called Josh first when he was kicked out of OAI, Rippling CEO called Neil first when the SVB crisis happened and he wanted to make sure every company could make payroll. Thrive was the CFO for GitHub which enabled them to preempt (with a pretty predatory structure actually) and gain a massive stake in the company because they were trusted to step in during a time of crisis. There’s a perennial demand for capital allocators who have great reputations that can stick their brand onto risky assets to make themselves to make them green and smooth the ecosystem out. 


In terms of brand, when I think about thrive, theirs really has a cult of personality around Josh. Josh has made himself a singular human that everyone wants to be associated with, and he understands the power of branding and reputation more than anyone else given that his own father had his reputation cancelled after going to jail early in his life. I think reputation-maxxing is what makes thrive the firm that it is: associating itself with the best CEO’s of our time, trying to make less investments and to be highly concentrated (or to at least give off that impression, as the firm rarely publicizes its deals so it’s unclear exactly how many investments it makes) so that it seems selective, and of course leaning into companies and ‘doing the right thing’ and always being there to build maximally good reputation.
