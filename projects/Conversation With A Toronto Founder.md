Things he brought up that I love:
- Capital is still underrated as a weapon for startup success. I think it's a bit dirty to talk about how much Capital matters because most founders are engineers first, and capital raising is usually associated with marketing or salesmanship. People think pitching to VCs is mostly bullshit; they feel the process is unfair and that VCs don’t judge them on the things that matter. They feel VCs look for signals and markers of success instead of deeply understanding the domain, the market, or whether a product is positioned to win.

These negative interactions have tainted the fact that capital is a big reason one company wins over another. The only companies that succeed out of the U.S. are those that are completely bottom‑up and capital‑light. In Canada, Shopify was a bottom‑up play: if you build something people really want, with obvious high ROI, people flood toward it. Internationally, companies like JetBrains are similar examples. Outside the U.S., building wealth often depends on being close to capital, which is a major determinant of success.

Capital also matters for enterprise sales, which is capital‑intensive. It requires hiring experienced executives and a sales force, doing extensive outbound outreach, hosting many calls, and running ad campaigns. It also involves costly activities like hosting conferences—inviting champions from different companies, covering travel, and providing a venue can cost a million dollars, but it helps build relationships that lead to deals. Enterprise departments have heavy risk and compliance needs, so you must hire risk and compliance staff.

Another consequence of capital’s importance is why many companies go to the Valley. Raising a Series A (often $10 million +), the round that really matters, is much easier in the U.S. Investors pattern‑match heavily: they see most successful startups coming from there and invest accordingly. Few Canadian companies have raised large Series A rounds, except during the Zerp period when investors were also looking internationally. Europe is far from North America, and for Canada the question is why not the U.S., given its cultural homogeneity, proximity, and the fact that many Canadians move there. Investors may view staying in Canada as an adverse selection signal, thinking founders aren’t willing to go all‑in, move to SF, and become “slaves for shareholders.” I underestimated how much this pattern‑matching drives investor capital allocation.

It's funny hearing him talk about discovering how enterprise sales really works. He would say, I thought enterprise sales was just about a product. I talked to people; they buy it. In reality, it took a billionaire who could wine and dine many people, and that got my product distribution. He seemed shell‑shocked and said that Canada usually lacks the talent density and advisor network to teach us how to bypass these enterprise processes. That's a big part of why Canada stalls in growth—not because of a lack of engineering talent, but because of missing talent in marketing, sales, and because most large enterprises are in the U.S., where the capital is.
