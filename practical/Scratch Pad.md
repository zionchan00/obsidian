		- The Crisis:

		- SOE thesis
			- 1. Post-war stagnation: In many countries, SOEs were seen as a solution to economic stagnation following World War II. Governments took control of key industries to drive growth and development.
			1. National security and strategic interests: SOEs were established in sectors considered vital for national security or of strategic importance, like natural resources, infrastructure, and utilities.
			2. Natural monopolies: In sectors with natural monopolies like infrastructure and utilities, governments created SOEs to prevent private monopolies and ensure public access.
			3. Protecting national resources: Resource-rich nations used SOEs to maintain control over valuable natural resources and prevent foreign exploitation.
			4. Social and political objectives: SOEs were used to pursue social goals like job creation, regional development, and providing affordable goods/services.
			5. Economic crises: During economic downturns, governments sometimes nationalized struggling private companies to prevent job losses and economic disruption.
			6. Ideological reasons: In some countries, particularly those with socialist leanings, SOEs were seen as preferable to private ownership on ideological grounds.
			7. Lack of private capital: In developing economies, SOEs were established where private capital was insufficient to develop key industries.
			8. 1. Post-2000s trend reversal: After decades of privatization, many developing economies started bringing the state back into the economy in the early 2000s.
			9. Economic power: SOEs now account for about 1/5th of global revenues, comparable to 1980s levels.
		1. What's the opportunity?
			1. 1. Rising global debt: Governments are facing fiscal pressures and may struggle to continue funding inefficient SOEs.
			2. Pressure from institutions: Organizations like the IMF are pushing for reduced state involvement in competitive markets.
			3. Governance issues: Many SOEs lack transparency and suffer from political corruption, hindering growth and innovation.
			4. Inefficiency: SOEs often underperform compared to private counterparts, with issues like overemployment and misaligned incentives.
			5. Fiscal burden: Governments increasingly recognize the strain SOEs put on public finances, especially as debt servicing costs rise.

- The players in the ecosystems: banks, federal reserve, ‘investors’, and companies. The key is to understand that banks, investors, and companies are always trying to figure out where their money will make the most amount of money, aka where they will get the highest return/yield
    

- Companies: they have lots of money, and they need to figure out what to do with it. Two options: keep it in a bank account, or you can buy bonds on the bond market, usually government bonds, called. Why are they attractive? Return/profit and safety
    

- Option 1: If you keep your money in a bank account, the bank will take that money and make investments with it, and share some of the money they make from the investments with you - which is better than NOT keeping your money in a bank account and keeping it in a warehouse, because then you don’t earn any money. What happened in 2008 was that the Banks did this by investing in really risky mortgages, and when the prices of homes went down, they lost a lot of peoples retirement money which they had kept in their bank accounts. The US government made no rules so that wouldn’t happen again, but yea that was scary, banks are more careful now. Generally banks are safe, your money wont’ just disappear from the account of a big bank like Citi or BofA, and there’s even insurance from the US government to protect it in in case it does. So it’s safe
    
- Option 2: But you could also buy bonds! Bonds also pay you money. The way a bond works is that when a company, or a government, wants to get money that they don’t have. Maybe they want to buy a company, maybe they want to take that money and use it to build a new product, or to launch in a new country. So they’ll try to get that money from investors, in what’s called a bond issuance. E.g $1M, 1M bonds, each one is a $1. And the company will make a promise that by the ‘maturity-date’  - which could be up to 10 years away, they will pay back the initial amount, called the principal, that $1. But also, for a regular time period, they’ll also pay a certain % of that principal, called interest. It could be every 2 months, every half a year, every year. And investors make money from that interest, that’s the profit
    

- Now the biggest set of bonds on the markets are ones from the US treasury. That money is then taken to be used for new spending by the USs government - new highways, for the military, new programs, etc. But as i said before, there are also corporate bonds. The nice thing is that the bond market for government bonds is big, with lots of buyers and sellers, which means if you want to buy or sell, you usually don’t have to wait a long time before someone agrees to buy from you ro sell to you, so if you have an emergency, you can get cash back pretty fast. So it’s also safe, and there’s a clear return, and if the US government were to default on their bonds, the world would really be over, so that scenario is super unlikely
    

- Banks: one option that banks have which investors and companies do NOT have is they can earn money by lending to each other. This is called the ‘federal funds rates’. When people talk about ‘interest rates’ or ‘rates’ going up or down, what they really mean is that the federal fund rate is going up or down
