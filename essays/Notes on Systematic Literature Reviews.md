		- For this evolution, you should read the FDA guidance documents directly rather than summaries. Start with:
			- ICH E8 (General Considerations for Clinical Trials) and E9 (Statistical Principles) for the international harmonization framework
			- FDA's "Providing Clinical Evidence of Effectiveness for Human Drug and Biological Products" (1998) which codifies what counts as adequate evidence
			- The 21st Century Cures Act (2016) text itself, which formally expanded what counts as evidence (real-world data, biomarkers, etc.)	
			- Tufts Center for the Study of Drug Development (CSDD) produces the most-cited empirical work on development costs and timelines—Joseph DiMasi's papers on R&D costs are foundational, though controversial (industry-funded, criticized for methodology). Read them skeptically but read them, because everyone in pharma cites them.
			- And Jack Scanlons stuff
		- For the failure mode perspective, Donald Light and Joel Lexchin's critical work is useful (e.g., "Pharmaceutical research and development: what do we get for all that money?" BMJ 2012). They argue industry cost estimates are inflated and that genuine innovation has declined relative to me-too drugs. You don't have to agree with their politics to find the empirical critique valuable.
		- Ben Goldacre's _Bad Pharma_ (2012) covers the systematic distortions in evidence generation—publication bias, trial registration failures, selective outcome reporting. It's polemical but grounded in actual data. His subsequent work on the AllTrials campaign and OpenTrials database shows what transparency interventions look like.
	- For HEOR, there's an actual canonical textbook: Drummond et al., _Methods for the Economic Evaluation of Health Care Programmes_. It's the methodological bible—cost-effectiveness analysis, QALY calculation, modeling approaches. Dense but foundational. Pair it with NICE's Methods Guide (free, publicly available), which shows how an HTA body actually applies these concepts to coverage decisions. Reading a few real NICE technology appraisals after that will click everything into place—you'll see how HEOR teams construct submissions and where the Evidence Review Groups tear them apart.
	- For Clinical Development, there isn't a single great book because the knowledge is distributed across FDA guidances and ICH guidelines. ICH E6 (Good Clinical Practice), E8 (trial design), E9 (statistics), E10 (control groups) are the actual governing documents. They're dry but short. Supplement with FDA's disease-specific guidances for whatever therapeutic areas your customers work in—these show what endpoints and trial designs the agency will accept for oncology vs. cardiology vs. rare disease, which vary enormously.

			- Method:  You define algorithms up front: how you infer indication, how you infer lines of therapy, washout periods, and what counts as discontinuation/switch. A big “edge case” is misclassification: codes don’t always reflect true diagnosis/severity, and therapy lines are messy (dose interruptions, off-label use, step edits). Good reviews explicitly test sensitivity analyses on these definitions.
			- Decision making: This review tells you what comparator(s) you must beat, what endpoints matter in practice, and which subpopulations have the highest unmet need or the clearest adoption pathway. It also feeds _budget impact_ and _market sizing_ (eligible treated population, not just prevalent population).
		- Unmet needs with a condition. 
			- “Unmet need” is not just “disease is bad.” It is a structured claim that **current care fails** on outcomes that matter (patients/clinicians/payers), or fails for identifiable subgroups, or is infeasible/unsafe/inaccessible.
			- Input: what counts as evidence. The review usually triangulates four kinds of evidence: (1) clinical outcomes under SoC (residual risk, non-response rates, relapse), (2) safety/tolerability and discontinuation, (3) patient-reported burden (symptoms, functioning, treatment burden), and (4) system constraints (access barriers, monitoring burden, infusion capacity, adherence complexity).
			- Data sources :RCTs and meta-analyses show efficacy ceilings and safety issues; RWE shows effectiveness gaps, adherence, and disparities; qualitative research (patient/clinician interviews, preference studies, discrete choice experiments) establishes what outcomes are actually valued and what tradeoffs are acceptable; guidelines/HTA reports reveal what decision-makers already believe is missing.			
			- **Criteria and methods.** You pre-specify the stakeholder lens (patient vs clinician vs payer) because “unmet need” differs by audience. You also explicitly define the counterfactual: “unmet need relative to best available therapy in region X for subgroup Y.” Without that, unmet-need claims become hand-wavy.
			- **How it’s interpreted in decision-making.** Unmet-need syntheses shape _positioning_ (for whom is the product “must-have”), _label strategy_ (which subgroup first), and _endpoint hierarchy_ (what you must improve to justify premium pricing or favorable reimbursement). In HTA, unmet need often influences acceptable uncertainty and can affect how benefits are weighed (but only if it’s evidenced and subgroup-specific, not generic).
		- Threshold analysis. **.** This is the most conceptually distinct: you’re not primarily estimating “what is,” but establishing **what size of change should count as meaningful**—clinically, to patients, and to payers—given baseline risk and alternatives.
			- Inputs: what you're trying to conclude: You typically need one or more of: minimal clinically important difference (MCID/MID) for a continuous endpoint; responder definitions for categorical interpretation; acceptable benefit-risk tradeoffs; and sometimes “target effect sizes” for powering trials (which is a _strategic_ threshold, not necessarily the patient-meaningful one).
			- **Data sources**: Anchor-based MCID studies (linking change in a scale to a patient global impression or functional milestone) are highest value; distribution-based estimates (SD, SEM) are supportive but weaker alone. Patient preference studies can define what tradeoffs people accept (e.g., toxicity vs symptom relief). Regulatory and HTA precedent matters: prior approvals, label claims, and what HTA bodies accepted as clinically relevant in past appraisals.

			- The vital role of a literature review in clinical research is to identify gaps in the current knowledge base. By reviewing existing literature, researchers can identify areas where there is a lack of evidence or inconclusive evidence. This informs the researchers’ ability to develop relevant research questions and hypotheses, thereby guiding the research design. A systematic review, for example, assists in determining whether an intervention’s effects have been studied among different populations or in different clinical contexts; a systematic review will provide the information the researchers require to more directly narrow their research (Mathew, 2022).

- The review stage is critical for identifying the best research design in clinical research. For example, a review of the literature may show that RCT design has been used effectively in prior studies to test the effectiveness of a drug, which suggests to the researcher that they should use that same design (Page et al., 2021).

## 3. Enhancing Study Rigour and Reducing Bias

Reviews of the literature are key to increasing rigour in clinical studies. When researchers can critically review previous studies, they actually review the quality and relevance of these studies to their own project. This is especially important when distinguishing between potential sources of bias and variability that could influence outcomes in the present study. For example, systematic reviews of diagnostic accuracy studies can provide helpful information regarding some types of methodological issues researchers can address (Whiting et al., 2004).

In addition, literature reviews can help in minimising bias in clinical studies by providing a balanced perspective of the evidence. As an outcome of synthesising studies utilising different methodologies with the quantitative and qualitative studies of evidence, researchers account for variance in perspectives, making findings more reliable and validated. This is particularly true when considering outcomes of complex studies in clinical studies regarding different features of the intervention, with participants having study-specific factors that may not apply to a separate study (Noyes et al., 2019).
