I fucking hate meetings.

It's very, very hard for me to take companies that make them longer than half an hour seriously. Because this means their employees learn and ship so slowly that the meeting doesn't feel like a comparative waste of time, because anyone who thinks fast definitely feels this. Or they do feel this way and they lack the spine to speak up. Or their culture is so oppressive against negative feedback that they either feel its pointless or they'll be actively punished for going against consensus. In any case, I don't want to work there. 

But this also means I haven't met a single startup where I really do want to work, because all of them have fallen into this trap. Given how none of them have passed, I should take a step back and think whether my opinion on meetings is correct. I'm just going off my emotions after being in a company with 4+ hour meetings.

I'm very pro conversations, even long ones, because they're 1-on-1. Some of the most important ideas in my life have been created during long conversations. But as soon as it becomes more than 2 people, I'm against them. Why? Because your mind naturally converts the benefits of a 1-on-1 conversation and falsely applies them to the setting of a meeting.
- Talking to another human is so engaging. one of the best ways to discover the truth. You're getting information beyond just the words of their voice - posture, facial expression, tonality, the pauses. It's so context heavy and information dense, and also you're forced to pay attention because it's just you two
	- This is not true in a meeting. In a meeting you can't just go deep into one persons ideas, because that means you're ignoring the rest of the team. The rest of the room also has the inability to maintain eye contact with that person, so there's a lack of physical engagement. 
- The quality of information is in your control becauseof the intimacy of conversation. In a 1-on-1 someone's honesty with you is dependent only on the relationship between you two and you guys as individuals. In a team setting people have to consider far more variables and hence they're much more likely to deviate from their true opinion. This also means that the more complex the topic and the more there is at stake, the less you want to make it a meeting. 

What do companies have meetigns for?
- Announcements - 1 person speaking to many others. OK these are fine
- Collecting information from people, no decisions made - everyone in the team giving updates, what's usually called a standup
- Collecting information from people, debate among people will happen, 1 person makes decisions  - 'product' meetings, 'sprint planning', 'what went wrong' meetings

I'm going to start with the last because now that I've listed the taxonomy the last is the one I hate the most because they can run into the 4-6 hour time band. Why?
- They're long because they're trying to do 3 jobs in 1
- Collecting info from people - for the reasons above, 1-on-1 is just better
- Debate - 1-on-1 trumps it for true information discovery, but if 
- The person makign the decision needs time to mull, and yet is being forced to think.


The first two aren't intrinsically bad - the first just has to be time capped proportional to how good the speaker is at speaking, the second is ok as long as it's short and informal (I don't know why eating dinner and asking 'what did you do today' is marginally better than the actual team standup where we go in a circle and it's pretty boring, but actual team standups are ok as long as each person is information-dense as fuck and bringing things up which are relevant to you all),

Consensus is the mind killer. Your brain turns off and agrees. This is good for a few minutes, actively painful for anyone of intelligence for longer than 10-15 minutes. 

But if we were to imagine no meetings at all, that also doesn't feel good. I think meetings are bad for info discovery because of conformity, but they're great at conformity also. So they should be used for announcements, when you want to celebrate something or announce something bad to the group, to reinforce the group identity that the group together is experiencing something. But I'd refrain from talking about anything that could fracture the group in a meeting. This means news about competitors, customers, funding, employees joining or leaving. 



 Because most people use meetings to discover the thought process of others, but strong social pressure exists to conform with that many bodies in a room so people very rarely speak their minds. 

Why does this matter? Because in startups given how long the work hours are, if most of your interactions with co-workers are through meetings, you frankly don't have an accurate sense of how they feel or what their true opinions are on topics. You're setting yourself up for a preference cascade.

What meetings are actually good for then is consensus-building. 

But I'm here to build a generational company. One that lasts decades. And frankly the only kind of culture which can incubate such a company is one that's highly generative, one that isn't afraid to even destroy the existing business in order to grow it multiples larger. That can only be a company where ideas that people hate to hear, ideas that people will yell about, thrive.
