
- One of my core beliefs on love was challenged last week by a friend. I had always thought that the feelings of deep love - constant thinking that you're the luckiest person in the world, the seemingly unending feeling of happiness, an experience more ecstatic than any of Shulgin's creations - automatically came with one's first relationship. I had thought that those magical feelings came from the inexperience and idealism that comes with the first relationship - that *the one* had been found, The permanent closing of the first relationship ends that dream. But he proved me wrong - some people enter their first relationship without believing that they are the one. The idealism is not automatic, meaning it might be possible for me to feel that feeling again. Why did I forget it in the first place? Should I want to feel it again?
- My feeling that I had found *the one* was not rational. I didn't even feel that way towards later partners who were better fits, who cared more, and who carried a higher probability of long-term success than my first match. It's not rational because when the feeling has me in its grip, how could I rationalize my luck? I felt that it was pure chance which led me to meet this person out of any of the other thousands of people I could have met, *the one* who was destined to be my lifelong partner. It is the singularity of the relationship that creates this most singular feeling. If you read this and tell me I'm too romantic and unrealistic, I tell you that you should be more romantic, to dream more
- My romanticism did not last; part of growing up it seems is to have your dreams dashed before your eyes. The one I thought was *the one* turned out not to be *the one*. Hoping to cheer me up, my friends told me that I'll eventually forget the way I felt, that I'll realize this relationship was flawed and not as special as I had thought, that there'll be others who are an even better fit. To move on I dismissed the uniqueness of my bond, that we may have had something irreplaceable, because there are so many potential partners out there. To move on I devalued the *idea* of a relationship as something I only ever have with *one* person. There was no more magic to being my partner - I could find many others out there to fit the role. Inward coping strategies notwithstanding, one's external environment only further damages the sacredness of the relationship. All around me people ended their relationships and seemed to be just as content on the inside with new ones. Their past failures having turned them into cynics, they deride anyone who dreams of finding *the one* as 'overly-attached' and 'unhealthy'. Logging onto social media platforms deluges the mind with hundreds of attractive faces with similar interests, thousands of options, a number so great that any one individual no longer seems special. Reflexivity kicks in and people only become less serious and more degrading of the sanctity of the relationship. The end result of the commodification of love is that acts which were formerly reserved for the love of your life are now open to people who you merely find physically attractive, of which there an unlimited source exists. Degradation begets more degradation; realists point at rising divorce statistics and the above mentioned degeneracy as evidence that romanticism is a false dream. Never has it ever been harder to dream of an extremely special someone, a very different someone, a someone who is *the one*.
- I stand against this and believe in an economics of love and its related acts, with the end goal of attaining the feeling once again. Even the most promiscuous humans still have some things they will not do and some people they will not do things with. They have standards because they cannot deny that there are some who are worthy of being bonded with them and some who aren't, regardless of how the logic behind the standards. But that the standards exist - this seems undeniable to me. If the standards for one act is higher than the standards for others, the fact that fewer are worthy of the act means that act is more sacred than the other. If we take all the acts of the relationship and lower their standards as moderns do, is it any surprise that most relationships are mundane, chore-like, *unmagical*? And even if the things we do with our partners have no intrinsic value, nothing in their nature to suggest that they are connected with love, I will artificially limit their supply to give them value, to create a new meaning in them. The feeling I describe above is **that** compelling
- I know that this feeling does not guarantee success in my future partnership. Being deeply in love does however make the relationship more a relationship, love become more lovelike than ever. To me it is one part of the best relationship but not the whole. To attain it one has fight convention and raise their standards on love and its most intimate acts by creating a near-impossible filter. Never to express romantic interest physically or verbally to anyone without already falling in love with them. This one person you will have known intimately for a long period of time. An appreciation for all the details of their being, of its uniqueness, will have been gained. It is only then when you know everything about them that they are worthy of being your partner.
- This advice may seem unrealistic given how few people will pass through these filters. Perhaps, only one. It's easy to forget from the marketplace of humans that is social media, endlessly introducing new humans for us to meet, that we only need to get it right with one person. I hope we all not only find that one person, but also experience a love so strong that only chance could be the cause of this miraculous pairing.
