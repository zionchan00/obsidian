	- The last blow to Fordism came from the rise of unregulated shareholder capitalism. The famous Friedman doctrine, which argued that the sole responsibility of a manager was to maximise the profits of the owners because the owners’ are the sole risk takers in providing capital for the business, evolved into simply maximising share price. Both pieces operated under the logic that share price was the best metric to judge managers on how well they led the company for long-term success. Executive compensation began to be tied to share performance in light of this philosophy, and today CEOs of the top 10 largest share repurchasers received 34% of their compensation from stock options and 24% in stock awards.
	- Unfortunately the regulatory environment has failed to foresee how the behaviour of investors and management compensation being tied to share price created negative externalities for the rest of society. The first negative externality is that asset-heavy businesses broadly speaking decrease in attractiveness. Modern financial theory posits that the value of a company is based on the sum of their present and future cash flows, discounted by a rate that represents the risk of not receiving those cash flows and the opportunity cost of not investing in alternative opportunities. Investors apply a larger discount rate to asset-heavy companies because their cash flows are higher risk in two ways. When the economy deteriorates or in case of inventory oversupply, dollars have to flow towards maintaining those physical assets even when they do not produce revenue. These fluctuations can cause a company to miss the forecasted financial goals that they publicly set, depressing the share price in the short-term, and face a much larger risk of bankruptcy in the long-run in times of recession.
	- In contrast, asset-light companies who earn their revenues through products created from human-capital such as software, branding, patents, etc, are able to capture the majority of the profits in the value chain while avoiding the downsides of asset-heavy businesses. Investors as a result overwhelmingly favour asset-light businesses over asset-heavy businesses. The way this manifests in the stock market is that investors are willing to pay many multiples more for the future cash flows of a human-capital company than a company which earns its dollars from physical assets. Companies which are a mix of asset-heavy and asset-light will see activist investors clamour for them to separate the two businesses into two separate companies so that the asset-light business receives the proper valuation of an asset-light business.
