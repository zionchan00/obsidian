[[Personal Journal]] 

It's really hard for me to think about being ambitious or competitive, because as soon as I do, it turns into: if I'm competitive, then I want to think I'm better than other human beings. I was abused a lot by my parents when I was young, and I think that left me believing I don't necessarily have a place in this world, that I don't deserve one. I don't operate from a place of intrinsic safety or intrinsic value. I feel like I have to earn my place. And as a consequence, when I interact with people, I never want to make them feel that way. I always want other people to feel like they have a place in the world, that they belong, and that they're good.

My theory is that most competitive people carry some intense insecurity that makes them need to prove they're better than others. They're also insecure about not knowing their place in the world, but they react to that insecurity differently than I do. I've reacted in the opposite direction, and I think that's holding me back a good amount. I'm not as ambitious as I could be. I'm not able to challenge others when I should, because of this fear inside me. It's a limiting factor in my life.

What I'm trying to figure out is how to get myself to see that if I watched another person in my situation, I'd find my views kind of ridiculous. It can make complete sense to want to win, to believe other people are worse than me at something, to challenge others, to move through the world with the idea that other people don't always know what they're doing, even when they have the best intentions. That if someone in a position of authority challenges me, I should hold my ground. That someone who has been doing something for a long time and has a lot of status maybe shouldn't be trusted automatically, and I should try to do things myself.

Money is also really hard for me to think about. There are a few parts to it. I'm unfamiliar with numbers and math, so I don't want to think about the quantitative side of anything. And when I think about trying to make money, it feels like a limitation being placed on me. I'm generally against the idea of responsibility or being forced to do something, and money is a hard limit on what you can do, so I try not to think about it. I try to pretend budgeting isn't a real thing. I hate chores, tasks, and recurring responsibilities like paying credit card bills, because it feels like I'm being forced to do things I don't like, and that reminds me of being oppressed by my parents. I understand that's not what's actually happening, but I still can't get past it. I can feel my eyes start to close whenever I think about it, even now, and I don't want that to happen. I'd like to be able to move through this freely, but it seems so hard.

And I'm not honest with myself about money, because the truth is I love spending. I love buying nice things. I love paying for my friends' stuff. I'd like to live in a nice house or a hotel. I'm nowhere near as Spartan as I think I am, and I don't actually want to compromise on those things. Some of this is about ambition and being capable, but mostly it's that I don't plan for my future until I have to. There's something about avoiding planning for the future that I quite like.

**My analysis**

The thing I keep noticing is that in both halves of this, a value you hold is sitting directly on top of an avoidance, and that's what makes the avoidance so hard to move. Kindness is sitting on top of not competing. Freedom is sitting on top of not budgeting. So any attempt to compete or budget feels like an attack on kindness or freedom, and you back off. I don't think either of those values is the problem. I think they've been recruited as cover, and the first job is to separate them from the thing they're covering.

Start with competition. You've welded two things together that don't actually belong together: being better than someone at something, and being worth more than them as a person. That weld is your parents' weld. In a home where worth is conditional, winning and losing really are statements about whether you deserve to exist, and you learned that lesson correctly for that environment. But you then applied it outward. You assume that if you beat someone, challenge them, or disagree with them, you'll be doing to them what was done to you. Most people are not built that way. Most people can lose an argument, be told they're wrong, or be outperformed, and their place in the world doesn't wobble at all, because they never had it made conditional. By treating everyone as if they're as fragile as you were made to be, you're projecting your own wound onto people who don't have it, and there's a quiet condescension hiding in that: it assumes the people around you can't handle an honest counterpart. Holding your ground against an authority figure who's wrong isn't a statement that they don't belong. It's a statement that you do.

I'd also gently push on your theory that competitive people are mostly running on insecurity. Some are. But a lot of competitiveness is play, or curiosity, or simply wanting the thing to be done well. And notice what the theory does for you: it lets you not compete while feeling morally ahead of the people who do. That's a competition too, just one you've arranged to win by default. I say that with affection, because it's a very understandable move, but it's worth seeing that the drive isn't absent in you. It's been rerouted into a channel that costs you nothing and gets you nothing.

On the reframe you're reaching for, I think you're aiming at the wrong target. You don't need to get yourself to believe "other people are worse than me." You'll never believe it, and you don't need to. The much smaller target is: "I'm allowed to be right, and my being right doesn't take anything from anyone." Which, notably, is exactly the thing you already extend to everyone else. You want others to feel they belong and are good; you've just exempted one person from the policy. The asymmetry is the whole issue. And there's a sharper point underneath: not challenging people is often not kindness. If you see a flaw and stay quiet because someone has tenure or status, you're letting a bad decision stand and withholding the one thing you could actually give them. The people you want to protect are usually better served by an honest colleague than a deferential one.

Money is three different things tangled into one knot, and they come apart more easily than you'd think. The first, unfamiliarity with numbers, is just a skill gap. It's mundane and fixable, and it's doing a lot of work as an excuse for the other two. The second is the one that matters: recurring obligations feel like coercion, coercion feels like your parents, and your body shuts down. That eyes-closing sensation you describe is a real, recognizable trauma response, not a character flaw, and it's the kind of thing that insight alone doesn't fully dissolve. Working on it with someone who does this kind of work would probably move it faster than thinking harder about it.

But here's the reframe I'd offer on the third part, the pleasure of not planning. You experience avoiding money as freedom. I'd argue it's the opposite. If you refuse to budget because budgeting feels like being controlled, your parents are still setting the agenda; they've just moved from telling you what to do to telling you what not to do. Real freedom would look like indifference: doing the boring thing because you want what's on the other side of it, with no charge on it either way. And you told me what's on the other side. You love nice things. You love paying for your friends. You want the good hotel. Those aren't compromises to your values, they're your values, and money is simply the mechanism for the generosity and pleasure you already care about. You were honest enough to notice that the Spartan self-image was a story that made the avoidance look virtuous. That's the same pattern as the competition story, and you caught it yourself, which is a good sign.

If I had to compress all of it: you're not someone who lacks ambition or doesn't care about money. You're someone who learned that wanting things and claiming space were dangerous, and then built two very decent-sounding philosophies to justify never doing either. The philosophies are the thing to retire, not the kindness or the love of freedom underneath them.
